Alternating liver and caul fat on skewers protects the organ meat from drying and creates the contrasting colours implied by the name.

## Ingredients

* 250 g caul fat, rolled and tied — or lamb or pork back fat as a substitute  
* 500 g lamb liver, cut into 4 cm pieces  
* 1½ tsp rock salt, ground very fine  
* 1 tsp ground black pepper

## Method

1. Roll the sheet of caul fat up into a cylinder about 3 cm across, tie it at intervals with butcher's twine, and cut it into 4 cm lengths. If you are using back fat, cut it into 4 cm pieces instead.  
2. Alternate liver and caul on metal skewers.  
3. Grill over medium-hot charcoal, turning frequently, until the caul is browned and the liver is just cooked through. Keep the skewers away from the hottest part of the fire — rendering caul drips and flares.  
4. Grind the salt and pepper together very fine, sprinkle generously over the skewers, and serve immediately.

**Notes**

* *Kālakhaṇḍa* is the liver, and securely so — Apte glosses it simply "the liver" and the Shabda-Sagara derives it as *kāla* "black" plus *khaṇḍa* "part". The chapter's own anatomy list settles it from the inside: 1425 runs *kālakhaṇḍaṃ tathā vṛkkau*, "the *kālakhaṇḍa* and the two kidneys", so it is a named organ distinct from the kidney, and the neighbouring dish at 1488–91 puts it alongside intestine, fat, and plain meat in a preparation called *pañcavarṇī*, "the five-coloured". A previous version of this note called the identification uncertain; it isn't.  
* **The fat is *vapā*, the caul or omentum, not trimmed fat.** Three things fix this. The chapter has an ordinary word for fat, *medas*, which it uses freely (1436, 1489, 1495) and does not use here. 1426 lists *vapā* among the parts *susaṃlagnaṃ*, "closely attached", inside the animal's body, and groups it with the brain and the marrow — soft internal tissues, not trimmings. And 1499 calls it *varti-baddhā*, "bound into a roll", wrapped with a band: a lacy sheet has to be rolled and tied before you can cut skewer-sized pieces from it, whereas back fat is already solid. *Vapā* is also the cut the Vedic animal sacrifice roasts on a spit, so a spitted omentum is an old idea in this literature. Back fat is a fair substitute, but it is a substitute.  
* **The size measure sits on the caul, and the liver matches it.** 1499 gives the caul roll *aṅgula-dvaya-mānena*, "by the measure of two finger-widths" — about 4 cm at the standard *aṅgula* of roughly 1.9 cm — and 1500 then cuts the liver *tat-pramāṇāni*, "of that measure". This is the one absolute measurement in a chapter that otherwise sizes everything by comparison (chickpea at 1449, jujube at 1453, areca nut at 1473 and 1502, camphor lump at 1462).  
* *Antarā*, "in between", is what makes this an alternating skewer rather than a mixed one, and *varṇa* in the dish name is colour: the point is the dark liver against the white caul, as in the *pañcavarṇī* of 1491.  
* 1500c–d, *śūlapotāni kṛtvā tān aṅgāreṣu pratāpayet*, recurs verbatim at 1464 in the published *Bhaditraka Skewers*. It is the chapter's standing formula for skewer-and-embers, so *śūlapota* is a real word in this witness rather than a slip for *śūlaprota*.  
* Do not overcook liver; the caul should render enough to baste it.  
* Two things in the Devanagari need checking against Shrigondekar's printed edition. 1499a, *pañcāṅgapaṭṭadveṣṭyā*, is 7 syllables against *anuṣṭubh*'s 8, and *dveṣṭyā* is not a form — it probably conceals something built on *veṣṭ-*, "to wrap". Reading *pañcāṅgula-* ("five finger-widths") for *pañcāṅga-* would restore the count exactly and would fit a verse that measures in *aṅgulas* two pādas later, but that is a conjecture and the Devanagari here is left as transcribed. The other five half-lines of the passage all come in at exactly 16.  
* 1501 closes *varṇitaṃ somabhūbhujā*, "described by King Soma" — the author signing his own recipe. Someśvara III wrote the Mānasollāsa, so the third person is a convention, not a citation of a source.
