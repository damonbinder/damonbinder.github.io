A short-dried, heavily salted meat subsequently roasted. For safety, the modern version uses refrigeration and a low oven rather than ambient shade-drying.

## Ingredients

* 1 kg lean beef, venison, lamb, or goat, cut into long strips 5 mm thick  
* 30 g fine salt  
* ½ tsp asafoetida dissolved in 50 ml water  
* 1 tsp ground black pepper  
* 1 tsp ground cumin

## Method

1. Toss the strips thoroughly with the salt, asafoetida-water, pepper, and cumin. Refrigerate for 12 hours.  
2. Arrange on racks and dry in a dehydrator or convection oven at 65–70°C until the surface is dry and the strips have lost about half their weight, 4–8 hours.  
3. Refrigerate or freeze until needed, and use within a week. They are not cooked through at this stage and are not safe to eat as they are.  
4. To serve, bury them in a heap of embers, or roast over charcoal or in a 220°C oven, until hot, browned, and cooked through.

**Notes**

* Do not reproduce the source’s one-to-three-day ambient drying without a validated curing process.  
* This reconstruction produces a semi-dried meat for prompt refrigeration, not shelf-stable jerky. Half the weight gone is nowhere near shelf stability, which needs something closer to two-thirds; and there is no nitrite, no acidification, and no smoke here. The safety of the dish rests entirely on step 4, so the strips must be roasted through before they are eaten, not nibbled off the rack.  
* **The text asks for fatty meat and this reconstruction uses lean.** 1513 opens *samedaskāni māṃsāni*, "meats with their fat", and the reconstruction departs from it deliberately: rendered fat oxidizes fast and there is no cure here to protect it. The text's own second version at 1516 switches to *hariṇa*, deer, which is naturally very lean, so lean meat is at least inside the passage. **Flagged** — if you want the fatty version, eat it the same day.  
* 30 g on 1 kg is 3%, up from 2.5%, towards the *prājya-lavaṇena* ("with abundant salt") of 1516. The pepper and cumin are a reading of *sambhāra*, "requisites, seasonings", which is the only thing 1516 says about them; the passage names no spice but asafoetida and salt.  
* 5 mm strips rather than 1 cm: at 1 cm the drying time runs long and the surface case-hardens before the middle gives up its water. The text says only *dīrghāṇi*, "long", and specifies no thickness.  
* ***Cakkalīs*, not "cut thin".** 1516 says the deer pieces are made into *cakkalīs*, the same term the chapter defines at 1436 as *tāḍapatra-samākārāḥ*, palmyra-leaf-shaped — long, flat, tapering slices. It is the shape used by the four published pork *Cakkalikās* and by 1505, and it is exactly the geometry you want for drying.  
* Three claims about the dish were dropped from the earlier translation and are worth keeping, because two of them are technical rather than decorative. 1515 calls the *upakhaṇḍakas* *sarvaśākottamāni*, "the best of all *śākas*" — *śāka* is normally a vegetable dish in this chapter (1548 subdivides it into fruit-, leaf-, tuber-, flower-, and pod-*śāka*), so this is either a loose use for "side dish" or a deliberate boast that a dried meat beats the vegetable course. 1517 calls them *hṛdyān pathyān sugandhāṃś ca*: *pathya* is the Ayurvedic category of the dietetically wholesome, the opposite of *apathya*, and not a synonym for "nice".  
* *Upakhaṇḍaka* is not in the lexica. It appears to be this text's own coinage — "sub-piece" — and is left transliterated for that reason.  
* All 10 half-lines of 1513–1517 scan clean at 16 syllables.
