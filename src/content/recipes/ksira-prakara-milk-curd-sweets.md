Fresh curds set with sour buttermilk are kneaded with rice flour, fried, and syruped. The result falls between a fried paneer sweet and a rice-flour fritter. Setting the milk with *takra* rather than with lemon matters: it gives a softer, springier curd that kneads and fries better than the firm, granular one an acid like lemon or vinegar produces.

## Ingredients

* 1 L whole milk  
* 400–600 ml well-soured buttermilk, or the sour whey from a previous batch (see notes)  
* 50 g rice flour  
* 100 g ghee, for shallow-frying  
* 150 g sugar  
* 150 ml water  
* ½ tsp ground cardamom

## Method

1. Bring the milk just to a boil. Lower the heat and add the soured buttermilk gradually, stirring once after each addition, until the curds separate clearly from a greenish whey. Take the pan off the heat and leave it to stand for 5 minutes.  
2. Strain through cloth, rinse briefly with cool water, and hang for 30 minutes.  
3. Knead the curds with the rice flour until smooth. Shape into small discs, balls, or rings — the text asks for various forms rather than one.  
4. Cook the sugar and water for 5–7 minutes to make a light syrup; add the cardamom.  
5. Shallow-fry the shapes in ghee over medium-low heat until golden, then transfer directly to the warm syrup.  
6. Soak for at least 30 minutes before serving.

**Notes**

* **Use genuinely sour buttermilk.** The text says *takram amlaṃ*, and it is marked sour for a reason: supermarket cultured buttermilk sits around pH 4.5 and 200 ml of it will not drop a litre of hot milk past the coagulation point — you get a flocculent mess rather than a clean break. Leave it at room temperature for 12–24 hours first, or use the sour whey saved from a previous batch, and expect to need roughly half the milk's volume.  
* **Lemon juice is a fallback, not an equivalent.** It sets a firmer, grainier curd. If the milk refuses to break, add lemon juice by the teaspoon, but the texture will be closer to paneer than to what the text describes.  
* *Śāli-taṇḍula-piṣṭa* is the flour of the fine white rice whose eight varieties open this chapter at 1345–48, so plain white rice flour is the target — not glutinous and not the parboiled *idli* kind.  
* At 50 g flour to roughly 200 g of curd this is a firm, fritter-like mixture. The text gives no proportion; half as much flour makes something closer to a chhena sweet, and correspondingly more fragile in the pan.  
* **"Cooked sugar" is defined by the next recipe.** *Pakva-śarkarā* here is glossed at 1413: at the soft stage the syrup is fluid and drinkable, at the middle stage honey-like, at the hard stage firm. A soaked sweet wants the soft stage, which is what a 5–7 minute syrup gives.  
* The source's *sarpiṣā paripācitam* is a thorough all-round cooking, which points at frying submerged in ghee. Shallow-frying is more economical and reduces the chance of disintegration.  
* One litre of whole milk yields around 200 g of curd, so about 250 g of paste and ten to twelve small pieces.  
* The dish's name comes from the following verse (1411), not from this one: *kṣīraprakāra-nāmedaṃ bhakṣyaṃ*, "this *bhakṣya* is named *kṣīraprakāra*."
