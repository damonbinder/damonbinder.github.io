There is no method here at all — two long lists of things, and the instruction *lavaṇāmbhasi*, "into salt water". The first list runs to aromatics, pods, and medicinal fruits; the second to mangoes, gourds, grapes, and eggplants. Several of the aromatics named — cardamom, asafoetida, pepper, fenugreek — are as likely to be flavouring the brine as being pickled in it, and the grammar does not distinguish the two.

## Ingredients

* 1 kg mixed firm produce: green mango, small eggplant, lime, *āmalaka* (gooseberry), cucumber, luffa, ash gourd, karonda, tindora, grapes, or tender lablab (*sem*) pods  
* 1 L water  
* 100 g non-iodized salt  
* Optional: ½ tsp fenugreek seed

## Method

1. Wash the produce and leave small items whole; cut larger items into 3–4 cm pieces.  
2. Dissolve the salt in the water by boiling, then cool completely.  
3. Pack the produce into a clean jar with the fenugreek if using, and cover fully with brine, using a weight to keep it submerged. Cover the jar loosely — a sealed jar will build pressure.  
4. Ferment at cool room temperature for 3–7 days, tasting daily, until the brine has definitely turned sour, then refrigerate.

**Notes**

* *Niṣpāva* is lablab or hyacinth bean, *Lablab purpureus* — Indian *sem*, *avarai*, *vāl* — and the part eaten is the young pod, whole. Not fava, and not green beans, which are a New World plant. Flat romano beans are the nearest widely available substitute.  
* *Bṛhatīdvaya*, "the two *bṛhatīs*", is the standard pairing of *Solanum indicum* and *S. xanthocarpum*, and *surasādvaya* in the leaf salad at 1551 works the same way. The chapter treats these thesaurus pairs as single list items.  
* Three items on the first list are worth knowing about. *Marica* here is green peppercorns, which brine very well; *karīra* is *Capparis decidua*, the desert caper whose buds are still a standard Rajasthani brine pickle; and *ketaka* is the screw pine, whose tender white heart is eaten.  
* Salt is the whole method, and the amount matters. 100 g of salt against 1 L of water and 1 kg of produce comes out at about 5% salt in the water phase once the produce has given up its own water, which is enough for a reliable lactic fermentation. A historical *lavaṇāmbhas* pickle would have run much higher — 10% and up in the water phase, preserving by osmotic dehydration rather than by souring, and needing a soak in fresh water before it could be eaten.  
* This makes a refrigerator pickle, not a preserve. Keep everything submerged and check that the brine actually sours. Discard the batch if it develops mould, putrid odour, or unusual colours.  
* Mustard is not in these five verses. It belongs to the next recipe, 1559–1560, which is an oil-and-mustard pickle rather than a brine.
