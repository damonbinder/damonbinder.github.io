*Kṛṣṇapāka* is "the black cooking": a mutton stew given its colour and its body by fresh blood stirred in at the end. The recipe is practical only where inspected food-grade blood is legally available.

## Ingredients

* 750 g boneless mutton or lamb shoulder, cut into 2 cm pieces  
* 200 ml food-grade lamb or pig blood  
* 200 ml tamarind water  
* 100 ml pomegranate juice  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* 1 tsp ground coriander  
* ½ tsp turmeric  
* ½ tsp ground dried ginger  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 1½ tsp rock salt, plus ½ tsp for the blood  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper  
* ¼ tsp ground cardamom  
* Optional: a speck of food-grade edible camphor  
* To finish: 20 g ghee, ¼ tsp asafoetida, ½ tsp cumin seeds

## Method

1. Whisk ½ tsp salt into the blood, then work it through your fingers or a whisk and strain it through a fine sieve, discarding the fibrous threads that gather. Cover and keep refrigerated.  
2. Combine the meat, tamarind water, pomegranate juice, cumin, coriander, turmeric, dried ginger, asafoetida, and 1½ tsp salt. Simmer covered until the meat is tender.  
3. Uncover and reduce until the liquid just coats the meat — 150–200 ml or so. Take the pan off the heat and let it drop below a simmer.  
4. Whisk a ladleful of the hot cooking liquid into the blood to temper it, then stir the tempered blood back into the pan.  
5. Return to low heat and cook, stirring constantly, until the sauce thickens and darkens and the blood reaches 71 °C / 160 °F on a probe — 5–10 minutes. Keep it at a bare tremble; if it boils, the blood granulates.  
6. Bloom the finishing asafoetida and cumin seeds in the hot ghee and pour them over. Scatter the pepper, cardamom, and optional camphor. Serve straight away.

**Notes**

* **Food safety, concretely.** Blood is a raw animal product and an excellent bacterial medium, so buy only blood sold for human consumption from a licensed butcher or abattoir, keep it at or below 4 °C, and use it within a day or two of purchase. Never taste it raw. It must reach 71 °C / 160 °F throughout — check with a probe rather than judging by thickening, since blood sets well below a safe temperature. Serve immediately and don't reheat the dish more than once. Retail sale of blood for eating is restricted or unusual in some countries, including much of the United States.
* **Why it wants to curdle, and what stops it.** Blood proteins begin setting around 65–70 °C and granulate above roughly 80 °C, and acid drops the temperature at which they aggregate — so adding cold blood straight into a hot, strongly sour pot is the reliable way to get a grainy sauce. The source's own two blood steps are functional answers to this: the pinch of salt at 1469 slows clotting, and kneading out the *sirā-jāla*, the "net of fibres", removes the fibrin that would otherwise show up as strings. Reducing the sour liquid first (step 3) and tempering the blood (step 4) are modern additions in the same spirit.
* **The reduction is inferred, not stated.** *Stokāvaśeṣa-pāke* (1474) means "when only a little of the cooking remains", i.e. when the dish is nearly done — it is about time, not about liquid. But the sister recipe at 1468 dries its pot out completely, and the blood needs a modest amount of liquid to disperse into rather than a soup, so reducing first is the reading that works.
* **The powders are the ones from 1449**, via a double *pūrvavat* at 1473 — turmeric, cumin, pepper and the like, dried ginger, coriander, and asafoetida. Note that the list already contains a pepper, so the pepper scattered at 1475 is a second one.
* **The only salt the text names is the pinch in the blood** (*lavaṇaṃ manāk*, 1469), and it is there to keep the blood liquid rather than to season it. Neither the 1449 powder list nor 1469–75 seasons the meat with salt; the 1½ tsp here is a modern addition.
* **There is no ghee in the passage.** *Dhūpayed hiṅgu-jīrakaiḥ* (1474) is a fumigation with asafoetida and cumin smoke; blooming them in hot ghee is the standard stand-in used elsewhere in this collection, and the ghee comes along with it. The dry scatter of camphor, cardamom, and pepper (1475) is a separate, later step.
* **The carcass verses are translated but not reconstructed.** 1469–72 begin at the throat-cut and cover bleeding, skinning from the chest with a knife-point, and boning out the cuts surveyed earlier in the chapter (*pūrvoddiṣṭa-pradeśeṣu*, 1472). None of that can or should be reproduced outside a regulated slaughter.
* 1471's first half runs one syllable over *anuṣṭubh*, so the skinning verse may have drifted in transmission; the sense is clear enough but the wording is not certain.
