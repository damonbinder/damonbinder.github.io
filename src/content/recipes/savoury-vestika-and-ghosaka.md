Two dishes from one seasoned chickpea, and the difference between them is the whole point. The *veṣṭikā* is wrapped up in a wheat dough-ball and cooked on an earthen griddle. The *dhosaka* has no wrapper at all: the same seasoned pulse goes straight onto a griddle smeared with oil, which is why the oil is specified here and nowhere in the *veṣṭikā* clause. That makes this passage the earliest description of what is now the dosa — and made from pulses alone, with no rice anywhere in it. Black gram, cowpea, or dried peas may stand in for the chickpea in the *dhosaka*.

## Veṣṭikā

### Ingredients

* 200 g split chickpeas (chana dal)  
* 15 g fresh ginger, finely chopped  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 1 tsp rock salt  
* 300 g *maṇḍaka* dough  
* 20 g ghee, for the griddle

### Method

1. Cook the chana dal until tender, drain very well, and mash coarsely.  
2. Mix with the ginger, cumin, asafoetida, and salt. Cool completely.  
3. Divide the dough and filling into 8 portions. Enclose the filling and roll each into a 12–15 cm round.  
4. Cook on a medium-hot earthenware griddle or heavy tawa brushed with ghee, 2–3 minutes per side, until browned in spots and cooked through.

## Dhosaka

### Ingredients

* 200 g split chickpeas (chana dal), or urad dal, cowpeas, or dried peas  
* 15 g fresh ginger, finely chopped  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 1 tsp rock salt  
* Oil, for the griddle

### Method

1. Soak the dal for 4–6 hours, drain, and grind with just enough water to a smooth pourable batter, about the consistency of thick cream.  
2. Stir in the ginger, cumin, asafoetida, and salt. Use it straight away — the text gives this batter no resting or souring, unlike the *iḍerikā* batter six verses later.  
3. Heat a tawa or heavy frying pan over medium heat and smear it with oil.  
4. Pour on a ladleful and spread it out from the centre into a thin round. Cook until the underside is set and freckled, oil the top, turn, and cook the second side.  
5. Serve hot off the pan.

**Notes**

* **The dish is *dhosaka*, not *ghosaka*.** Shrigondekar's printed text has `धोसकान्` in both halves of 1393, with no variant recorded in his apparatus; the transcription in the `original` field above has `घोसकान्`, a `घ`/`ध` confusion. *Dhosaka* is the Sanskritized form of the word behind Tamil *tōcai* and Kannada *dose*, and Shrigondekar says as much in his own introduction, listing "Dhoṣa" and "Iḍali" among the chapter's dish names still current in Kannada and Tamil. The secondary literature accordingly reads this passage as the earliest recipe for the dosa. **Recommended title on publication: *Savoury Veṣṭikā and Dhosaka*.**  
* **`हरिमन्थ` is chickpea, and is not uncertain.** It is a standard lexical synonym of `चणक` (*Cicer arietinum*), given in Monier-Williams and in the Ayurvedic nighaṇṭus. The reason the poet reached for the longer word is metrical: `हरिमन्थस्य विदलं` fills the pāda at eight syllables where `चणकस्य विदलं` would leave it one short. So the *veṣṭikā* and the *dhosaka* are made from the same pulse, and 1392's `विदलं चणकस्यैवं` is not introducing a second one. An earlier version of these notes called the word uncertain and suggested mung.  
* **What the text does and does not specify about the pulse.** `पूर्वसम्भारसंस्कृतम्` sends you back to 1391's seasonings — asafoetida, cumin, salt, ginger — and nothing further. Neither dish gets a boiling or grinding verb of its own; the chapter supplies those two verses earlier, at the *pūrikā*'s `उत्क्वाथ्य विदलान् पिष्ट्वा`. [Judgement call: boiled-and-mashed for the *veṣṭikā*, because a raw ground filling will not cook through inside a bread in two minutes a side, and soaked-and-ground for the *dhosaka*, because a boiled paste will not spread. The text distinguishes only the wrapper and the vessel.]  
* **`खर्पर` and `ताप्या`.** The *veṣṭikā* is cooked on a `खर्पर`, an earthenware pan or potsherd; the *dhosaka* on a `ताप्या`, the hot plate that gives modern *tavā*. The chapter treats them as alternatives for one job at 1383, so the real distinction between these two dishes is the dough, not the metal.  
* **`वट्टाण` is the pea**, *Pisum sativum* — the Prakrit form behind Marathi *vāṭāṇā*, and one of the several Deccan vernacular words this chapter carries. `राजमाष` is the cowpea or black-eyed pea, *Vigna unguiculata*; despite the modern Hindi *rājmā* it is not the kidney bean, which had not reached India.  
* `वेष्टिका` is from *veṣṭ*, to wrap. The *Skandapurāṇa* uses the same word for fried savouries coiled into loops, but here the instrumental `गोलकेन` names what does the wrapping, so this one is enclosed in dough.  
* Also collated: 1394c reads `वट्टाणकस्य` in the printed edition, not `वट्टाणस्य` as transcribed above, which is why that half-verse currently scans a syllable short.
