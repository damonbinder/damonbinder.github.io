A very rich cultured curd made from milk concentrated to half its volume before inoculation. The starter is *takra* — the churned, watered buttermilk the text does not define until 1572, three verses later, and which is itself made by churning this curd. The chain loops, the way a kept-back culture does.

## Ingredients

* 1 L whole milk  
* 30 g plain live-culture yoghurt or cultured buttermilk  
* Optional: 20 g sugar  
* Optional: ¼ tsp ground cardamom

## Method

1. Reduce the milk gently to 500 ml, stirring often.  
2. Cool to 42–45°C. Whisk in the yoghurt or buttermilk and the optional sugar or cardamom.  
3. Pour into a clean container, cover, and incubate undisturbed at about 40°C for 8–12 hours, until set and pleasantly tart. Expect it to take noticeably longer than a plain-milk set at the same temperature.  
4. Refrigerate immediately.

**Notes**

* **The starter is *takra*, not generic buttermilk.** *Takram īṣad vinikṣipet*, "one should throw in a little *takra*" — and *takra* is defined three verses later at 1572 as churned curd cut with a quarter of its volume of water. So the chain is circular: this curd gets churned into *takra*, and a spoonful of *takra* sets the next batch. The chapter uses *takra* as a dairy seed elsewhere too; at 1409 sour *takra* is the coagulant that curdles milk for *kṣīraprakāra*.  
* **Six *yāmas* is not obviously too long.** A *yāma* is a watch of roughly three hours, so eighteen hours. There is no need to reach for a different time reckoning to explain it: milk reduced to half carries about double the casein, double the lactose, and double the buffering capacity, so substantially more acid has to be produced before the pH falls to 4.6 and the gel sets. At an ambient 25–30 °C, with no more than a spoonful of *takra* as inoculum, eighteen hours is a plausible set. The text gives you the shorter option itself: *hīnakāle tathā pathyam*, "in a shorter time it is likewise wholesome".  
* **It will set firm and shed very little whey.** Simmering milk down to half far exceeds the 85 °C hold used on modern yoghurt milk, so the whey proteins are fully denatured and complexed with the casein, and the resulting gel is dense and resists weeping. That matters two recipes downstream — pressing this curd for *śikhariṇī* yields more strained curd and less whey than pressing ordinary yoghurt would.  
* Use live-culture dairy as the starter; boiling after inoculation will prevent setting.  
* Nothing in 1568–70 says to cool the milk before inoculating, or names a temperature. Both are modern. What the text does specify is a **new** pot (*nava-sthālī*) and a draught-free spot.  
* Restored to the translation: *pathyaṃ mṛṣṭaṃ manoharam*, the three epithets 1570 gives the finished curd, and *hīnakāle tathā pathyam*, which had been dropped along with them.
