The meat is already cooked when this recipe starts: 1476 opens with *aṅgārabhṛṣṭaka*, ember-roasted meat, laid on a clean board. What makes the dish is the cut — *tilaśaḥ*, to the fineness of sesame seeds, with the ginger and the *gṛñjana* cut to match.

## Ingredients

* 500 g cooked grilled lean meat: pork loin, lamb leg or loin, or goat  
* 50 g lemon or citron pulp, finely chopped  
* 15 g fresh ginger, finely chopped  
* 1 small garlic clove or 2 spring onions, finely chopped  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 1 tsp rock salt  
* 15 ml lemon juice  
* 15 g ghee

## Method

1. Chop the meat by hand until the pieces are about the size of sesame seeds.  
2. Mix with the citrus pulp, ginger, garlic or spring onion, cumin, pepper, asafoetida, salt, and lemon juice.  
3. Melt the ghee until hot and pour it over the mixture. Toss and serve warm.

**Notes**

* *Gṛñjana* may be garlic, onion, or another allium. Monier-Williams gives "a kind of onion or garlic or a small red variety of it", the Kannada lexica give garlic outright, and one Sanskrit-English gloss adds turnip. This chapter has settled words for garlic (*laśuna*, 1451 and 1460) and onion (*palāṇḍu*, 1451, 1454, 1460), so *gṛñjana* is probably a third, distinct allium rather than either of those.  
* Pouring hot ghee over asafoetida is a practical stand-in for the *hiṅgu-dhūpa* of 1478, which is literally asafoetida smoke — the same verse sets it against *hiṅgu-toya*, asafoetida water, which is the form the chapter reaches for when it wants the asafoetida *in* the food. The substitution is period-appropriate rather than modern: the chapter's own puryala (1459–60) heats ghee separately and throws asafoetida into it. To fumigate instead, stand a small cup of glowing charcoal on the salad, put the asafoetida on the coal with a few drops of ghee, and cover the bowl for five minutes.  
* **Use a lean cut.** The meat is cut *tilaśaḥ*, to sesame-seed fineness, which maximizes the surface area of any fat that sets as the salad cools — a fatty cut turns waxy in the mouth within a couple of minutes. The chapter discriminates: the *cakkalikās* take *medo-bhāgaṃ*, "the fatty portion" of the cooked *śuṇṭhakas*, while the bhaditraka and puryala both call for *śuddha māṃsa*, clean lean meat. The hot ghee poured over at the end is what carries a lean cut, so don't compensate with a fattier one.
* *Kesarāmla* (1477) is the citron under a second name, not a separate sour fruit — the same ruling as in the *Sour Citron Pork Cakkalikās*, where 1440 gives the identical triple of citron *kesara*, finely cut fresh ginger, and *kesarāmla*. So the verse asks for one citrus, not two.
* Citron is hard to find; lemon pulp with a little finely grated peel is the closest substitute. The 15 ml of lemon juice is a modern addition, making up for lemon being milder than citron.
* **The passage shares verse 1478 with the next recipe, and the seam runs down the middle of it.** 1478a–b (*miśrayitvā tu tanmāṃsaṃ hiṅgudhūpena vāsayet*) closes this dish — *tanmāṃsaṃ*, "that meat", points back to the ember-roasted meat of 1476a. 1478c–d then starts fresh with *āmaṃ māṃsaṃ*, raw meat on the grinding stone, and has no finite verb of its own until *peṣayed* in 1479; it belongs to the *Māṃsa-Vaṭaka* trio. Both recipes print the whole of 1478 and translate only their own half.
