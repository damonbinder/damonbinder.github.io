The verse opens with *athavā*, "or else": this is the second treatment for the same produce the preceding passage has just put into salt water, and the pickling section runs the two options in parallel from here on. The oil is not named, and unqualified *taila* in this register means sesame — the mustard arrives separately, as powder. Small fruits go in whole with their stalks still attached.

## Ingredients

* 1 kg green mango, bitter gourd, firm green banana, or young jackfruit  
* 80 g salt  
* 80 g ground mustard seed  
* 250 ml sesame oil, or mustard oil — see notes  
* Optional: 1 tsp turmeric  
* Optional: 1 tsp fenugreek seed

## Method

1. Wash and dry the produce completely. Leave small green mangoes whole with their stalks attached; cut bitter gourd, banana, and jackfruit into bite-sized pieces. Toss with the salt and rest in the refrigerator overnight.  
2. Drain any released liquid and spread the pieces to dry for 1 hour.  
3. Heat the oil until it just begins to smoke, then cool to warm.  
4. Mix the produce with the ground mustard, the optional turmeric and fenugreek, and the warm oil.  
5. Pack into a clean jar, pressing the solids down so that the oil stands a centimetre clear above them. Refrigerate and leave for at least 5 days before eating.

**Notes**

* *Athavā*, "or else", ties this back to the salt-water pickling of 1554–58: the same long list of fruits, pods, and gourds, packed in mustard and oil instead of brine. 1561–64 then offers both options again for shoots and roots.  
* *Vṛnta-sahitam*, "together with the stalk", is a deliberate instruction and worth keeping — an intact stem plug is what stops a whole green mango splitting and spoiling at the shoulder. Only the fruits of 1559 get it; the bitter gourd, jackfruit, and banana of 1560 carry no such qualifier.  
* **The oil is unnamed.** Unqualified *taila* in Sanskrit means sesame oil — the word is derived from *tila*, sesame, and the Ayurvedic convention is that any unspecified oil is sesame. The companion verse 1562 asks for oil *with mustard in it* (*sa-rājike taile*), which only makes sense if the oil is not itself pressed from mustard. Mustard oil is the living South Asian pickle medium and works well here, but sesame is what the text most likely means, so it leads the ingredient list.  
* Turmeric and fenugreek are not in 1559–60. Both are modern pickle-masala habits; turmeric appears elsewhere in the chapter but not here.  
* The source does not state heating the oil; this is a common modern treatment that mellows raw mustard oil.  
* **Salt and safety.** Green mango is acidic enough (pH under 3.5) to be safe on its own. Bitter gourd, jackfruit, and green banana are not — they sit near pH 5.5–6, and produce held under a layer of oil is anaerobic, which is the risk profile behind the garlic-in-oil botulism cases. At 8% salt with refrigeration this is a pickle to eat within a few weeks, not a shelf-stable preserve; traditional ambient-stable oil pickles get there with 10–15% salt and hard sun-drying.  
* Commercial mustard oil labelled for external use should not be used as food where regulations prohibit it.
