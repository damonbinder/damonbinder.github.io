The recipe uses two vessels, and the switch matters. The crabs go into very hot copper for a dry roast, then into an earthen pot for the sour cooking. Acid standing in unlined copper dissolves copper salts, which is reason enough to change pots.

## Ingredients

* 1 kg whole cleaned crabs or 750 g crab sections  
* 40 ml lemon juice or tamarind water  
* 1½ tsp salt  
* 50 g ghee  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper or long pepper

## Method

1. Heat a heavy dry pan until very hot — copper if you have it, and unlined copper is fine at this stage, because there is no acid in it yet. Add the crab and cook, covered, for 3–5 minutes, until the shells brighten and begin to crack.  
2. Move the crab to a clay pot, or to any non-reactive pan. Add the lemon juice or tamarind water and half the salt, cover, and cook until the crab is done — 6–10 minutes depending on size.  
3. Lift the crab out and let the sour liquid drain off. Heat the ghee in a wide pan, add the crab, and fry over high heat until glossy. Scatter over the cumin, pepper, and remaining salt.

**Notes**

* The verb at 1540d as transmitted is *vivarjayet*, "he should shun", which makes no sense here. *Vibharjayet*, "he should roast", is the obvious repair and scans identically; the chapter uses *paribharjayet* for exactly this operation at 1534, 1536, and 1549. Worth checking against a printed edition.  
* *Khapara* for the crab's shell is a vernacular form — Hindi *khappar*, a potsherd or a skull — rather than the Sanskrit *karpara*. The metre allows either.  
* The instruction to remove “hands and feet” may refer to cleaning or separating claws and legs rather than discarding all limbs.  
* 1542's powder list is closed — cumin, salt, and *tīkṣṇa*, with no *-ādi* — and it has no asafoetida, which almost every neighbouring recipe does. That absence looks deliberate, so there is none here either. *Tīkṣṇa* is a named spice in this chapter rather than a generic pungent, most probably black pepper.  
* 1542 ends with four dietetic epithets — *vṛṣya* (aphrodisiac), *balakara* (strengthening), *hṛdya* (good for the heart), *mṛṣṭa* (delicious). The first three are Ayurvedic property terms rather than praise.
