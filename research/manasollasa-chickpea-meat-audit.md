# Mānasollāsa 3.13.1449–1452 — the "chickpea" reading tested

*Created 2026-07-25 11:20.*

Scope: `src/content/recipes/chickpea-and-meat-mixture.md` (`status: draft`), against `src/data/sources/manasollasa.ts` and the neighbouring recipes 1442–48, 1453–56, 1457–61, and 1462–65. Nothing in `src/` was edited.

## Summary

**This is a dry-finished fry of finely diced meat with fresh green chickpeas and tender bean pods, cooked down in the sour curd sauce of 1442–48 until the liquid has gone.** There are no chickpea dumplings, nuggets, or paste in it anywhere; the only chickpeas in the dish are the green ones at 1450d, and they are a garnish-scale vegetable, not the body of the recipe.

**Damon's hypothesis is right, and I'd put it above 90%.** `चणकस्य समान् खण्डान्` (1449a) is a size specification — "pieces the size of a chickpea" — and the material is meat, understood from the chapter the way it is understood at 1453. The strongest argument is not the size idiom (which is real, and which I'll document) but a case-marking pattern internal to these four verses: **every ingredient the passage adds is in the accusative, and the passage's instrumentals mark what you add things *to*.** `श्लक्ष्णमांसैः` (1451a) is instrumental. So the "finely cut meats" are not being introduced — they are already in the pan, and they are what 1449 cut. The material reading has to explain why the dish's main protein arrives in the one case that everything else in the passage avoids.

The size-by-comparison idiom is emphatically real: I count **ten** instances of it in this stretch of the chapter, and `[size-standard] + खण्डान् + कृत्वा/कल्पयित्वा + विचक्षणः` is a formulaic line that recurs almost verbatim at 1502. Independently, `caṇaka-pramāṇa` is a *codified* unit of size in technical Sanskrit — it is how Ayurvedic pharmacy specifies pill size — so reading `चणक` as a ruler is the ordinary use of the word, not special pleading. The **graded ascending series** across 1449 → 1453 → 1457, though, is pattern-matching: it stops at 1462, and the chapter's dice sizes over the whole run do not trend. Say the idiom is standing; don't claim the series.

The reconstruction therefore needs rebuilding rather than patching. Its ingredient list invents a chickpea component that isn't there, cuts the meat at 2 cm when the text asks for about 1 cm, misidentifies `निष्पाव` (lablab pods, eaten whole and tender — not shelled fava beans), and mashes the recipe's actual organizing idea, which is that everything in the pan is cut to one small size. The notes' two hedges are both wrong: the shaped chickpea pieces need no hedge because they should go, and `कोल` is not "uncertain and omitted" so much as ambiguous between two readings that are both cookable.

## 1. The size idiom is the chapter's default recipe opening

Ten instances in ~150 verses, using four different comparison words (`सम`, `प्रमाण`, `सङ्काश`, `आकार`) plus the adverbial `-शः` and one absolute measure. Verse, phrase, and what it specifies:

Damon's three checks all hold. `घनसार` **is** camphor — Monier-Williams gives camphor first (also mercury, water, and a tree), the Pali and Kannada lexica agree, and the Kannada entry specifies fragrant camphor used in cooking. The chapter itself burns and scatters camphor as `कर्पूर` at 1437 and 1475, so a camphor lump is a familiar object for its audience. `बदराकारकान्` at 1453 and `स्थूलामलकसङ्काशान्` at 1457 are as described.

### The series is not real; the formula is

1449 (chickpea) → 1453 (jujube) → 1457 (large *āmalaka*) does ascend. But 1462 drops back to a camphor lump, 1473 and 1502 both sit at areca-nut, 1476 goes to sesame, and 1499 gives an absolute two finger-widths. Three ascending items in a row is what you get one time in six by chance, and the run doesn't continue. **Drop the graded-series claim.** It isn't needed, because the *formula* claim is much stronger:

- **1449a–b:** `चणकस्य समान् खण्डान् कल्पयित्वा विचक्षणः`
- **1502b:** `पूगीफलप्रमाणेन खण्डान् कृत्वा विचक्षणः`

Slot for slot: [size standard] + `खण्डान्` + [`कृत्वा`/`कल्पयित्वा`] + `विचक्षणः`, down to the same pāda-final word. And in 1502 the *material* — `कालखण्डं` — is named in the preceding pāda and left out of the formula, which is exactly the ellipsis I am claiming for 1449.

## 2. Three arguments that settle 1449a

### C1. The case pattern: accusative adds, instrumental accompanies

Everything the passage puts into the pan is accusative: `समानार्द्रकखण्डान्`, `चणकान् हरितान्`, `कोलं`, `निष्पावान् कोमलान्`, `पलाण्डुशकलान्`, `लशुनं`. The passage's two instrumentals both mean "together with something already in hand": `चूर्णितैः … आद्यैः … हिङ्गुभिः` (1449b–1450a, what the pieces are mixed *with*) and `श्लक्ष्णमांसैः` (1451a).

So `श्लक्ष्णमांसैः क्षिपेत्कोलं` is "along with the finely cut meats he should add *kola*" — the meat is the thing already frying, and `कोलं` and `निष्पावान्` are what join it. Under the material reading, the dish's protein would have to be introduced in the instrumental while a fruit, a bean, an onion, and a garlic clove all get the accusative. That is not a thing a competent author does.

`श्लक्ष्ण` is also a fixed term in this text for "finely divided": `मेदसः श्लक्ष्णखण्डानि` (1495, finely cut fat), `सुश्लक्ष्णं सैन्धवं` (1364, 1501, very fine salt), `श्लक्ष्णमांससमायुक्तं … ओदनम्` (1592, rice with finely cut meat — the same compound as here). Chickpea-sized dice **are** `श्लक्ष्णमांस`. Mashed chickpea nuggets are not.

### C2. Word order puts `चणकस्य` with `समान्`, not with `खण्डान्`

The chapter does use a genitive of material with `खण्ड`: `मूलकस्य च खण्डकान्` (1454), `शुद्धमांसस्य खण्डकान्` (1457), `खण्डानि कालखण्डस्य` (1500). In every one of those the material genitive sits immediately beside its head noun. In 1449a the word beside `खण्डान्` is `समान्`, and `चणकस्य` sits beside `समान्`.

1457 is the control, because it has both at once: `स्थूलामलकसङ्काशान् शुद्धमांसस्य खण्डकान्` — comparison phrase first, material genitive hugging `खण्डकान्`. 1449a has the reverse order, which is what you get when the genitive belongs to the comparison word rather than to the head.

Grammatically this is the ordinary construction. `सम` in the sense "equal to" takes the instrumental or, optionally, the genitive (Pāṇini 2.3.72 on `tulyārtha` words), and the chapter uses the instrumental variant at 1361 (`विदलैः सममानतः`). A genitive standard of comparison with `सम` needs no defending.

### C3. `caṇaka-pramāṇa` is a standing unit, not an ad-hoc comparison

In Ayurvedic pharmacy *caṇaka-mātrā* / *caṇaka-pramāṇa* — "chickpea measure" — is a codified dosage size for pills, standardized at roughly 2 *ratti* ≈ 250 mg; there is a published paper on nothing but its metric equivalent. So a 12th-century Sanskrit technical author reaching for `चणक` when he wants to say "about a centimetre across" is using the conventional ruler of his register. This is the point that turns Damon's reading from clever into expected.

### C4. Nothing in the passage makes a chickpea preparation

To get chickpea nuggets you have to import soaking, boiling, mashing, and shaping, none of which the text mentions. When this chapter wants a shaped pulse item it names one: `वटकान्` (1454, 1480), `गोलकानि` (1479), `घोसकान्` (1393), `कटकर्णान्` (1396). And when it wants split chickpea it says `विदलं चणकस्य` (1392, 1394) — genitive of material, with a pulse-specific head noun and no comparison word in sight. That contrast is the honest counter-evidence to Damon's reading, and it dissolves on inspection: the two `चणकस्य` instances that *are* material both attach to `विदल`, in the pulse chapter, without `सम`.

## 3. `चणक` twice in four pādas — this strengthens the case

1450d has `चणकान् हरितानपि`, "green chickpeas too", as a real ingredient. That does not weaken the reading; it tightens it, for two reasons.

- **The author disambiguated.** The measure is bare `चणकस्य`; the ingredient is marked `हरितान्`, green or fresh. Fresh green chana (*chholia*, *hara chana*) is a distinct seasonal vegetable from the dried commodity, and the dried commodity is what the size standard refers to. The qualifier is doing exactly the work a careful writer would want it to do.
- **The measure sense is lexicalized, so the double use is cheap.** Compare the alternative: under the material reading, `सम` has to shift sense inside four pādas — "uniform, equal to each other" at 1449a, then "equal [to those]" at 1450c, echoing `तत्प्रमाणान्` in the parallel at 1453c. One of the two words has to do double duty either way. It is far cheaper for `चणक` to alternate between a conventional unit and a marked ingredient than for `सम`, a function word whose sense is fixed by its construction, to change meaning mid-passage.

The chapter is anyway relaxed about synonym variation for the same substance — dried ginger is `नागर` (1373, 1416, 1457), `शुण्ठी` (1449, 1547), and `विश्व` (1445); turmeric is `निशा` here and `हरिद्रा` at 1444. A word used two ways four pādas apart is well within this author's tolerances.

## 4. What is being cut, and what the anaphora actually points at

**Plain meat, freshly cut — not the roast pork *śuṇṭhakas*.** The chain to 1442–48 is real, but it runs through the *sauce* and nothing else.

- 1449–50 seasons the pieces with raw spice powders and fries them in hot oil. That is a from-raw sequence. The *śuṇṭhakas* of 1427–35 arrive already roasted, already peppered and salted (1434a–b), and cut to squares off long strips — an order of magnitude bigger than a chickpea.
- **The text back-references when it wants to, and here it doesn't back-reference the meat.** 1447 says `प्रक्षिप्य शुण्ठकांस्तत्र`. 1436 says `स्विन्नानां शुण्ठकानां`. 1453 says `पूर्ववत्` — but attaches it to the *powders* (`पूर्ववच्चूर्णमिश्रितान्`), not to the meat. 1449–52 has no anaphoric marker on the meat at all: no `तान्`, no `शुण्ठकान्`, no `पूर्ववत्`.
- **1452's `पूर्वोदितं` is the sauce.** It is neuter accusative singular, and the available neuter antecedent is `प्रलेहकं` (1446). `शोषितेऽम्लरसे` in the next pāda — "when the sour juice has dried away" — confirms that the previously described thing is the sour preparation. So the correct rendering is "the cook should apply the previously described [sour preparation] as he likes", which is what the existing translation already says.
- **The parallel at 1453 leaves the material unstated too**, and Damon's own published *kavacandī* translation supplies "meat" there without comment. 1449 should get the same treatment, with the bracket convention: "pieces [of meat] the size of a chickpea".

For the record on the section's drift: 1427 names `वराह` explicitly; 1442–48 and 1449–52 name no animal; 1453–56 names none and invites `पलान्यन्यानि कानिचित्`, "some other meats"; 1457 and 1462 say `शुद्धं मांसं`; 1469 switches to `मेष`, sheep. 1449–52 sits in the generic middle. Lamb or goat shoulder is the safe reconstruction, with pork shoulder available if Damon wants to keep the pork thread from 1427.

## 5. `कोल` (1451b) — two-way, and both readings are cookable

The current note ("jujube is possible but does not fit the context securely") is too dismissive and gets the reason wrong. Damon's specific worry — that `बदर` at 1453 makes a second jujube word suspicious — does not survive contact with the lexica.

**Jujube.** `कोल` is not a loose synonym of `बदर` but a *grade* within it: the Ayurvedic dravyaguṇa triad is *sauvīra* (large, Z. sativa), **kola** (medium, Z. jujuba), and *karkandhu* (small, Z. nummularia). So the chapter using `बदर` as a size standard at 1453 and `कोल` for a specific fruit at 1451 is unremarkable. There is also a canonical Indic precedent for kola in a pork dish: `sampannakolakaṃ sūkaramaṃsaṃ` at AN III 49, standardly rendered "pork with jujube".

**Pepper.** Monier-Williams gives the neuter `कोल` three senses — jujube fruit, the weight of one *tola*, and **black pepper**. `कोलक` is glossed "black pepper" (MW), "long pepper" (Shabda-Sagara, Yates), and "black pepper, cubeb" (Böhtlingk–Roth); the Concise Pali dictionary has simply "pepper". And the *Rāja-nighaṇṭu* — 13th century, so within a century of this text — lists `kola` among the synonyms of *pippalī* (Piper longum), *marica* (Piper nigrum), and *cavya* (Piper chaba). Note also that `कोलं` is singular where the countables around it are plural, and that it patterns with the mass-noun `लशुनं`.

**Where I land.** Slight edge to jujube, on two grounds: this chapter has a settled word for black pepper (`मरिच`, used throughout, including in the sauce at 1445) and does not need a hapax for it; and 1449's powder list has already handled the dry spices, so a spice arriving loose in the middle of a vegetable list is out of sequence. But `कोलं` in company with tender pods, onion, and garlic is not a secure fruit either. **Recommendation: keep `*kola*` transliterated in the translation, offer dried jujube as an optional element in the reconstruction, and give the pepper reading in the notes** rather than declaring the word omitted.

Third possibility, for completeness: `कोल` masculine is attested as "hog", and the chapter's pork thread runs directly into this passage. I don't believe it — `वराह` is this text's pig word, and a bare `कोलं` for pork-meat is a stretch — but it is worth knowing the sense exists.

## 6. `निष्पाव` (1451c) — secure, and the current reconstruction has the wrong plant

**Lablab purpureus** (= *Dolichos lablab*), the hyacinth or lablab bean, Indian *sem* / *avarai* / *vāl*. Confidence: high. This is the standard identification across the lexica and the Ayurvedic literature, where Caraka places `निष्पाव` in the *śākavarga* and names the **pod** as the part eaten as a vegetable.

The chapter corroborates it twice. At 1365–66 `निष्पावा` are cooked **whole** (`अभिन्नाः`), without asafoetida or turmeric, as against the pulses that get split — so this is a bean handled as a vegetable rather than a dal. At 1558 `निष्पावं` appears in the brine-pickling list among pods and gourds. And 1451 marks them `कोमलान्`, tender: young pods, eaten whole.

So the reconstruction's "100 g cooked fava beans" is wrong on both the plant (*Vicia faba*, unrelated) and the preparation (shelled and cooked, rather than whole tender pods). Fresh *sem* pods are the target; flat romano or helda beans are the closest widely available substitute. `निष्पाव` recurs elsewhere in the chapter, so fixing it here fixes a term Damon will meet again — 1396 has `निष्पावचूर्ण`, lablab flour.

## 8. Transcription integrity

I scanned all 16 pādas of 1449–1452 against *anuṣṭubh*. **All 16 come in at exactly 8 syllables.** On the same diagnostic, 1442–48 scored 28/28 and 1427–35 scored 28/36, so this passage sits with the clean one.

Two consequences. First, `कोलं` is not plausibly a scribal doublet of the following `कोमलान्` — the metre needs it. Second, this recipe does not carry the caveat that the *Śuṇṭhaka* does; the Devanagari can go public without an edition check. I have not compared it against Shrigondekar's GOS text, so the caveat is that this is an internal-consistency check, not a collation.

## 10. Rewritten Ingredients and Method

Title: the current **Chickpea and Meat Mixture** is now actively misleading, and the text gives this dish no name (contrast *kavacandī* at 1456, *puryala* at 1461, *bhaditraka* at 1465). Suggest **Finely Diced Meat with Green Chickpeas**, or if Damon wants the dice foregrounded, **Chickpea-Sized Meat Fry**.

Intro prose, replacing "A mixed fry of chickpea pieces, fresh chickpeas, meat, beans, and aromatics…" — which restates the ingredient list and names an ingredient the dish doesn't have:

> The chickpea in 1449 is a ruler, not an ingredient: the text asks for meat cut to the size of a chickpea, and then for the ginger to be cut to match. That one instruction organizes the whole dish — everything in the pan is about a centimetre across, so it cooks fast and finishes as a dry, clinging fry rather than a stew.

### Ingredients

* 500 g boneless lamb or goat shoulder, cut into 1 cm dice
* 25 g fresh ginger, cut into 1 cm dice
* 150 g fresh or frozen green chickpeas
* 150 g tender lablab (*sem*) pods, topped and tailed, cut into 3 cm lengths
* 1 small onion, cut into slices
* 3 garlic cloves, sliced
* 30 g dried jujube, pitted and torn — optional, see notes
* ½ tsp turmeric
* 1 tsp ground cumin
* ½ tsp ground black pepper
* ½ tsp ground dried ginger
* 1 tsp ground coriander
* ¼ tsp asafoetida
* 45 ml oil
* 250 ml sour glaze sauce — one batch of the *Sour Meat Glaze*, made through step 5 and without the meat
* To finish: ¼ tsp asafoetida, ½ tsp cumin seeds, 1 tsp ghee

### Method

1. Toss the diced meat with the turmeric, cumin, pepper, dried ginger, coriander, and asafoetida until evenly coated.
2. Heat the oil in a wide pan until it shimmers. Fry the meat over high heat, stirring, until browned all over — about 5 minutes. Add the diced ginger and the green chickpeas and fry for 2 minutes more.
3. Add the lablab pods, the onion and garlic, and the jujube if using. Fry for another 2 minutes.
4. Pour in the sour sauce. Cook uncovered over medium heat, stirring often, until the meat is tender and the sour liquid has dried away entirely and clings to the pieces — 12–18 minutes. Add a splash of water if it dries before the meat is soft.
5. Bloom the finishing asafoetida and cumin seeds in the hot ghee and stir them through off the heat.
6. Taste before adding salt. The sauce carries all the salt the passage asks for.

Notes on the numbers. The 1 cm dice is the whole point, so it should be the first thing in step 1 and the reason the sequence differs from the neighbouring recipes — at that size the meat is quick, which is why it goes in first and hard rather than being browned and then braised. 45 ml of oil is a shallow-fry rather than a sauté, which is what `तप्ततैले विनिक्षिपेत्` asks for; the parallel at 1453 is `पक्वतैले विपाचयेत्` and at 1502 `तैलेनाभ्यज्य`. One full batch of the 1442–48 sauce reduces to roughly 250 ml, which dries down over 800 g of solids without needing a second reduction. **No salt appears anywhere in 1449–1452** — the sauce brings 1½ tsp of rock salt from 1445, and that should be enough.

One practical bonus worth knowing: this is the one place where the curd sauce's tendency to grain does not matter. The prior audit worried about the *Glaze* splitting under reduction; here the endpoint is `शोषिते`, dry, so the curd solids are supposed to end up as a coating on the meat. A slightly granular finish is what a dry fry looks like.

## 11. Notes the recipe should carry

Replacing both existing notes, which should go.

* **The chickpea is a measure.** `Caṇakasya samān khaṇḍān` (1449) is "pieces the size of a chickpea", not pieces made of chickpea — a genitive standard of comparison with *sama*, and the chapter's standard way of opening a meat recipe. It specifies dice by comparison ten times in this stretch: jujube-sized at 1453, large-*āmalaka*-sized at 1457, camphor-lump-sized at 1462, areca-nut-sized at 1473 and 1502, sesame-fine at 1476, two finger-widths at 1499. The line at 1502 — *pūgīphala-pramāṇena khaṇḍān kṛtvā vicakṣaṇaḥ* — is the same formula as 1449 with a different ruler and the material named in the previous pāda. *Caṇaka-pramāṇa* is anyway a codified unit in technical Sanskrit; it is how Ayurvedic pharmacy specifies pill size.
* **What confirms it inside the passage.** Every ingredient the passage adds is accusative; `ślakṣṇamāṃsaiḥ` (1451) is instrumental, so the "finely cut meats" are already in the pan rather than being introduced — which means the pieces of 1449 are meat. *Ślakṣṇa* is this text's word for finely divided, used of fat at 1495, salt at 1364, and meat again at 1592.
* **The green chickpeas are the real ones.** 1450 asks for *caṇakān haritān*, fresh green chickpeas — marked *harita* precisely because the bare word had just been used as a ruler. Frozen green chickpeas work; shelled edamame is the nearest substitute.
* ***Niṣpāva*** is lablab or hyacinth bean, *Lablab purpureus* (Indian *sem*), and *komala* means the young pods, eaten whole. The chapter cooks *niṣpāva* whole rather than split (1365–66) and pickles the pods in brine (1558). Flat romano beans stand in.
* ***Kola*** is ambiguous and both readings are cookable. It is most often the jujube — specifically the mid-sized grade of *badara* in the Ayurvedic triad, so no clash with the *badara* used as a measure at 1453 — and there is an old precedent for pork with kola at *Aṅguttara Nikāya* III 49. But Monier-Williams also gives neuter *kola* as black pepper, *kolaka* is glossed pepper or cubeb across the lexica, and the near-contemporary *Rāja-nighaṇṭu* lists *kola* among the synonyms of three *Piper* species. Dried jujube is the version here; a teaspoon of coarsely cracked long pepper is the other reading.
* **The sauce is the one from 1442–48.** *Pūrvoditaṃ* (1452) is neuter singular, matching *pralehakaṃ* at 1446, and *śoṣite 'mlarase* — "when the sour juice has dried away" — names the sour preparation as the thing that goes dry. So this dish is the second use the text makes of that sauce, after the roast pork *śuṇṭhakas* at 1447.
* **No salt in the passage**, and *tīkṣṇa* in the powder list is a named spice rather than a generic pungent, most likely black pepper; the *-ādi* after it leaves the list open.

## 13. What would falsify this

- **A different reading at 1449a in Shrigondekar's GOS edition.** Anything like `चणकांश्च समान् खण्डान्`, or a `चणकमांस-` compound, or `समं खण्डं`, would change the picture. The transcription in `manasollasa.ts` scans clean, which is evidence it hasn't drifted, but it is not a collation.
- **`श्लक्ष्णमांसानि` for `श्लक्ष्णमांसैः`.** An accusative there would restore 1451 as the first introduction of meat and reopen the material reading. It would also break the metre — `श्लक्ष्णमांसानि क्षिपेत्` is 9 — so I don't expect it, but it is the single variant that would do the most damage.
- **A published translation reading 1449 as a chickpea preparation on manuscript grounds.** I could not find any rendering of 1449–1452 online, in English or otherwise; the secondary literature on the chapter names *śuṇṭhaka*, *cakkalikā*, *kavacandī*, *puryāla*, *bhaḍitraka*, *kṛṣṇapāka*, and *kośali*, and this unnamed dish falls through the gaps. If Damon has access to the GOS volume or to Arundhati's *Royal Life in Mānasollāsa*, 1449–1452 is worth a direct look — but on the internal evidence the size reading is not close.
