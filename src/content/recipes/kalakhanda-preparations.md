Four dressings for the ember-roasted liver of the previous recipe: dry-spiced, mustard-coated, curd-and-mustard, and sliced into *cakkalikās* with citron. The fifth preparation, at 1506, is in the dual and is probably not liver at all — see the notes.

## Ingredients

* 600 g lamb or calf liver  
* 20 ml oil  
* 1 tsp ground black pepper  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* 1½ tsp rock salt  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida and 1 tsp ghee, for the finishing smoke  
* For the mustard version: 2 Tb mustard paste  
* For the curd version: 150 g yoghurt and 1 tsp mustard  
* For the citron version: 50 g chopped citron or lemon pulp  
* For the sour-fried version: 2 lamb kidneys, 200 ml tamarind water, and 20 ml additional oil

## Method

1. Roast the liver in large pieces over embers, as in the previous recipe, or pan-sear it, until browned outside and just cooked within. Rest, then cut into pieces about 2 cm across — the size of an areca nut.  
2. For the dry version, turn the pieces through the oil, then scatter the pepper, cumin, and salt over them.  
3. Bloom the asafoetida in the hot ghee and pour it over off the heat.  
4. For the mustard or curd versions, coat the warm pieces with the chosen dressing.  
5. For the citron version, cut the roasted liver into *cakkalikās* instead — long strips about 3 mm thick and 5–7 cm long — and toss them with the citron pulp and salt.  
6. For the sour-fried version, halve the kidneys lengthwise, salt them, steep them in the tamarind water for 30 minutes, drain, and fry in the oil until cooked through.

**Notes**

* *Kālakhaṇḍa* is the liver. Apte glosses it simply "the liver", the Shabda-Sagara derives it as *kāla* "black" plus *khaṇḍa* "part", and 1425 lists *kālakhaṇḍaṃ tathā vṛkkau*, "the *kālakhaṇḍa* and the two kidneys", which rules out the kidney and any other paired organ.  
* **The areca nut is a ruler, not an ingredient.** 1502c–d reads *pūgīphala-pramāṇena khaṇḍān kṛtvā vicakṣaṇaḥ*, "the discerning cook, having made pieces of areca-nut measure" — the material, *kālakhaṇḍaṃ*, is named in the preceding pāda and left out of the sizing formula. Areca nut is a betel-chewing nut and could not be the material here in any case. The same line recurs at 1449a–b as *caṇakasya samān khaṇḍān kalpayitvā vicakṣaṇaḥ*, slot for slot: size standard, then *khaṇḍān*, then an absolutive of "make", then *vicakṣaṇaḥ*. An areca nut is 2–2.5 cm, so that is the dice.  
* **This recipe chains off the previous one.** 1502a is *aṅgāreṣu tathā bhṛṣṭvā*, "having roasted over embers **in the same way**", pointing back to 1500's ember roast, and 1504a is *anena vidhinā*, "by this method". Neither back-reference survived the earlier translation, which is why the method used to start from a pan-sear.  
* ***Cakkalikās*, not "thin slices".** 1505b is *kṛtvā cakkalikāḥ śubhāḥ*, which is word for word 1436b. 1436 defines the shape — *tāḍapatra-samākārāḥ*, "of the same shape as a palmyra leaf" — so this is the liver version of the four published pork *Cakkalikās*, and it should be cut the same way.  
* **1506 is in the dual and the earlier translation pluralized it away.** *Samedaskau*, *dvidhā bhaktau*, *lavaṇamiśritau*, *tau* — four dual forms in a row, so not a scribal slip. Something paired, fatty, halved, steeped in sour, and fried. The kidneys are the obvious candidate: 1425 names *vṛkkau* in the dual immediately after *kālakhaṇḍa*, kidneys sit inside a shell of fat, halving them lengthwise is how you prepare them, and soaking them in something acidic before frying is exactly what the smell asks for. I'd put it around 75% and the reconstruction follows it, but the referent is not stated and the alternative is that these are simply fatty pieces of the liver. **Flagged: revert step 6 to fatty liver if you'd rather not commit.**  
* *Ajājī* is cumin, *Cuminum cyminum*. The *Rājanighaṇṭu* — thirteenth century, so within a century of this text — lists it among the synonyms of *śvetajīraka*, white cumin. Caraway would be *kṛṣṇajīraka* or *kāravī*. The chapter uses plain *jīraka* elsewhere (1495, 1542) and varies its synonyms freely, the same way it runs through *nāgara*, *śuṇṭhī*, and *viśva* for dried ginger.  
* *Hiṅgudhūpena dhūpayet* is fumigation, not a dry spice: asafoetida bloomed in hot fat and poured over, as the chapter's other perfumed dishes do.  
* Citron is hard to find; lemon pulp with a little finely grated peel is the closest substitute, as in the published *Cakkalikās*.  
* All 10 half-lines of 1502–1506 scan clean at 16 syllables, so the Devanagari here does not need the edition check that 1499–1501 does.
