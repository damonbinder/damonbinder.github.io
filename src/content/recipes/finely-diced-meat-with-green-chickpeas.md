The chickpea in 1449 is a ruler, not an ingredient: the text asks for meat cut to the size of a chickpea, and then for the ginger to be cut to match. That one instruction organizes the whole dish — everything in the pan is about a centimetre across, so it cooks fast and finishes as a dry, clinging fry rather than a stew.

## Ingredients

* 500 g boneless lamb or goat shoulder, cut into 1 cm dice  
* 25 g fresh ginger, cut into 1 cm dice  
* 150 g fresh or frozen green chickpeas  
* 150 g tender lablab (*sem*) pods, topped and tailed, cut into 3 cm lengths  
* 1 small onion, cut into slices  
* 3 garlic cloves, sliced  
* 30 g dried jujube, pitted and torn — optional, see notes  
* ½ tsp turmeric  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper  
* ½ tsp ground dried ginger  
* 1 tsp ground coriander  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 45 ml oil  
* 250 ml sour glaze sauce — one batch of the *Sour Meat Glaze*, made through step 5 and without the meat  
* To finish: ¼ tsp asafoetida, ½ tsp cumin seeds, 1 tsp ghee

## Method

1. Toss the diced meat with the turmeric, cumin, pepper, dried ginger, coriander, and asafoetida until evenly coated.  
2. Heat the oil in a wide pan until it shimmers. Fry the meat over high heat, stirring, until browned all over, about 5 minutes. Add the diced ginger and the green chickpeas and fry for 2 minutes more.  
3. Add the lablab pods, the onion and garlic, and the jujube if using. Fry for another 2 minutes.  
4. Pour in the sour sauce. Cook uncovered over medium heat, stirring often, until the meat is tender and the sour liquid has dried away entirely and clings to the pieces, 12–18 minutes. Add a splash of water if it dries before the meat is soft.  
5. Bloom the finishing asafoetida and cumin seeds in the hot ghee and stir them through off the heat.  
6. Taste before adding salt. The sauce carries all the salt the passage asks for.

**Notes**

* **The chickpea is a measure.** *Caṇakasya samān khaṇḍān* (1449) is "pieces the size of a chickpea", not pieces made of chickpea — a genitive standard of comparison with *sama*, and the chapter's standard way of opening a meat recipe. It specifies dice by comparison ten times in this stretch: jujube-sized at 1453, large-*āmalaka*-sized at 1457, camphor-lump-sized at 1462, areca-nut-sized at 1473 and 1502, sesame-fine at 1476, two finger-widths at 1499. The line at 1502 — *pūgīphala-pramāṇena khaṇḍān kṛtvā vicakṣaṇaḥ* — is the same formula as 1449 with a different ruler and the material named in the previous pāda. *Caṇaka-pramāṇa* is anyway a codified unit in technical Sanskrit; it is how Ayurvedic pharmacy specifies pill size.  
* **What confirms it inside the passage.** Every ingredient the passage adds is accusative; *ślakṣṇamāṃsaiḥ* (1451) is instrumental, so the "finely cut meats" are already in the pan rather than being introduced — which means the pieces of 1449 are meat. *Ślakṣṇa* is this text's word for finely divided, used of fat at 1495, salt at 1364, and meat again at 1592.  
* **The green chickpeas are the real ones.** 1450 asks for *caṇakān haritān*, fresh green chickpeas, marked *harita* precisely because the bare word had just been used as a ruler. Frozen green chickpeas work; shelled edamame is the nearest substitute.  
* *Niṣpāva* is lablab or hyacinth bean, *Lablab purpureus* (Indian *sem*), and *komala* means the young pods, eaten whole. The chapter cooks *niṣpāva* whole rather than split (1365–66) and pickles the pods in brine (1558). Flat romano beans stand in.  
* *Kola* is ambiguous and both readings are cookable. It is most often the jujube — specifically the mid-sized grade of *badara* in the Ayurvedic triad, so no clash with the *badara* used as a measure at 1453. But Monier-Williams also gives neuter *kola* as black pepper, *kolaka* is glossed pepper or cubeb across the lexica, and the near-contemporary *Rāja-nighaṇṭu* lists *kola* among the synonyms of three *Piper* species. Dried jujube is the version here; a teaspoon of coarsely cracked long pepper is the other reading.  
* **The sauce is the one from 1442–48.** *Pūrvoditaṃ* (1452) is neuter singular, matching *pralehakaṃ* at 1446, and *śoṣite 'mlarase* — "when the sour juice has dried away" — names the sour preparation as the thing that goes dry. So this dish is the second use the text makes of that sauce, after the roast pork *śuṇṭhakas* at 1447.  
* No salt appears anywhere in 1449–1452; the sauce brings 1½ tsp of rock salt from 1445. *Tīkṣṇa* in the powder list is a named spice rather than a generic pungent, most likely black pepper, and the *-ādi* after it leaves the list open.
