The chapter's ancestor of the idli, made from black gram alone with no rice in it. The batter is ground, beaten, and left for the day until it sours; it then goes into small moulds, is covered over with more moulds, and is cooked through. It is steamed, and served cold. This is the parent batter for the *ghārikā* rings of the next verse, so one grinding makes both.

## Ingredients

* 250 g skinned split black gram (*urad dal dhuli*)  
* 150–200 ml water for grinding, plus water for soaking  
* 1 tsp fine salt  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* ½ tsp ground cumin  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper  
* 20 g ghee

## Method

1. Soak the urad dal in plenty of cold water for 4–6 hours, then drain.  
2. Grind to a very thick, smooth, fluffy batter, adding the grinding water a little at a time. Aim for something that holds a soft peak rather than pouring.  
3. Beat the batter vigorously for 5 minutes to incorporate air — the text asks for this explicitly and repeatedly. Mix in the salt and half the asafoetida.  
4. Cover and ferment at 28–30 °C for 8–14 hours, until it has risen, smells sour, and tastes sour. Somewhere cool will take 16–20 hours. It should be sour, not merely bubbly.  
5. Line small ramekins or the cups of an idli tray with squares of damp muslin, or use a cloth-lined idli plate. Fill two-thirds full, cover each with a second mould or with the tray lid, and steam for 12–15 minutes, until a skewer comes out clean.  
6. Turn them out and cool completely — the verse calls the finished cakes `सुशीताः`, well cooled, and they are white and smooth once cold.  
7. Scatter the pepper over them and brush them with a little of the ghee. Heat the rest of the ghee until it shimmers, drop in the cumin and the remaining asafoetida, and pour it over.

**Notes**

* **They are steamed, and the printed text says so more clearly than the transcription above.** Shrigondekar reads 1399c as `वस्त्रगर्भाभिरन्याभिः` — "with others having cloth inside" — where the transcription in the `original` field has lost `वस्त्र-`, which is also why that half-verse currently scans two syllables short. So the covering moulds are *cloth-lined*, which is how idli moulds are still lined, and cloth over a wet batter means moist heat. Two further arguments point the same way. The finished cakes are `धवलाः`, white, whereas the same batter deep-fried in the very next verse comes out `लौहित्यं`, red — a colour contrast the author draws deliberately, and only moist heat keeps a pulse batter white. And the scholarly summary of this passage renders it as placed in moulds and "cooked by steam properly". `परिपाचयेत्`, "cook right through, all round", fits an enclosed vessel.  
* **`वैटिका` is the mould, and the word is not fully settled.** Shrigondekar prints `वैटिकासु` with no variant recorded, and the sense "in small cups or bowls" is secure enough from the locative plus the covering vessels — compare Marathi *vāṭī*, a small bowl. The secondary literature on this verse calls the vessels *piṭika* instead, and `पिटक` is a word this chapter does use for a vessel set over the mouth of a pot, at 1356. Kept transliterated because the exact form is uncertain; nothing about the reconstruction depends on which it is.  
* **No rice.** The batter is `माषपिष्ट`, black-gram paste, start to finish, and 1401 confirms it when it sends the same `माषपिष्ट` on to the *ghārikās*. Modern idli batter is two parts rice to one of urad; this is not that, and it ferments faster and tastes far more strongly of the pulse.  
* **The seasonings in the batter are unspecified.** 1398 says only `सम्भारेण विमिश्रितान्`, mixed with the seasoning-mix, and leaves the contents to the cook; the named spices — pepper, asafoetida, ghee, cumin — all belong to the finishing at 1400. Salt and a little asafoetida is a reading of `सम्भार`, not a translation of it. Salt before fermenting slows the rise slightly; adding it after the ferment is the common modern practice but is not what the verse does.  
* **The order at the finish is pepper, then ghee, then the tempering.** `अवतार्य` off the fire, `मरिचं चूर्णितं विकिरेत्` scatter the ground pepper, `घृताक्तान्` smeared with ghee, and then `हिङ्गुसर्पिर्भ्यां जीरकेण च धूपयेत्` — perfumed with asafoetida-and-ghee and with cumin. The plain ghee smear and the hot asafoetida ghee are two separate operations, and the pepper goes on first, not last.  
* **Fermentation, practically.** 250 g of dal takes up roughly 350 ml during the soak, so the 150–200 ml at grinding is on top of that; the finished batter runs about two parts water to one part dry pulse, which is thicker than idli batter and is what lets the cakes hold their shape in a shallow mould. Urad-only batter is a vigorous fermenter — the mucilage in the pulse traps the gas — so watch it rather than the clock.  
* The verse starts from `माषस्य विदलान्` still in their skins and rubs the husks off by hand after soaking. Modern *urad dal dhuli* is split and skinned already, so step 1 is the whole of `क्लिन्नान् निस्तुषान् हस्तलोडनैः`.  
* **A citation problem.** The first half of 1397, quoted at the head of the `original` field above, belongs to the *kaṭakarṇa* recipe, not to this one — it is the `यावद्` clause that finishes the frying instruction at 1396. This entry's own text starts at 1397c.
