Two verses of shoots and two of roots, run through the same pair of packs as the fruit before them: salt water, or oil with mustard. The shoots are asked for *tender* and then merely washed — there is no cooking step in the source at all. Several of the roots are vernacular names that no lexicon pins down, which is why the list below is shorter than the text's.

## Ingredients

* 1 kg edible shoots or roots: prepared bamboo shoots, asparagus, ginger-family rhizomes such as zedoary or mango-ginger, elephant-foot yam, or drumstick pods  
* For brine: 1 L water and 60 g salt  
* Or for oil pickle: 250 ml sesame or mustard oil, 40 g salt, 40 g ground mustard

## Method

1. Peel and cut the roots or shoots into bite-sized pieces.  
2. Boil in abundant water until just tender, then drain and dry completely. This step is a modern safety addition rather than a reading of the source — see notes. Bamboo shoots and elephant-foot yam must be thoroughly cooked according to established modern preparation guidance.  
3. For brine, pack into a jar and cover with cooled salt brine. Leave at room temperature for 2–3 days, then refrigerate.  
4. For the oil pickle, mix with the salt, mustard, and cooled heated oil. Pack into a clean jar so the oil stands clear above the solids, and refrigerate.

**Notes**

* ***Mṛdūn* is an adjective, not an instruction.** *Prarohān kṣālitān mṛdūn* is three accusative plurals in agreement: "the sprouts, washed, tender". The text tells you to take shoots that are *already* tender and wash them, and there is no cooking anywhere in 1561–64 — when this author means "having made them X" he writes *kṛtvā*, as he does at 1564. The boiling above is modern.  
* It is also necessary. Bamboo shoots contain cyanogenic glycosides and elephant-foot yam calcium-oxalate raphides, both of which need thorough cooking whatever the source says.  
* **Securely identified.** *Sūraṇa* is elephant-foot yam (*Amorphophallus paeoniifolius*). *Kacora* is zedoary (*Curcuma zedoaria*), and *kacora* is specifically the Kannada form of the name, which is what you would expect in a Kalyāṇa Cālukya text. *Amla-haridrakā*, "sour turmeric", is mango-ginger (*Curcuma amada*), still one of the standard South Indian pickling rhizomes. *Śatāvarī* is *Asparagus racemosus*, whose young shoots are eaten. *Madhu-śigru* is the sweet or red variety of the drumstick tree, given in the lexica as *Moringa concanensis*; it had been dropped from the translation altogether.  
* **Drumstick root.** The pungent part of the tree, and probably what 1564 wants, is the root — but moringa root bark contains spirochin and is not regarded as safe in quantity, so the pods stand in for it here.  
* **Not identified**, and left transliterated: *cakrī*, *pātāla*, *ṭeṇṭuka*, *māgiṇī* (three times), *paiṣṭu*, *bilakanda*. The best available leads, none of them secure: *cakrī* is glossed in the Indian plant lexica as *Senna obtusifolia*, sicklepod, whose tender monsoon shoots are eaten as a green; *ṭeṇṭuka* is probably *Diospyros* (tendu), which also appears in the brine list at 1556; *pātāla* may stand for *pāṭalā*, *Stereospermum suaveolens*; and Kannada *māgiṇi* is glossed as the *māgadhī* vine, that is, long pepper (*Piper longum*), whose root is a drug in its own right — which would make *karpūra-māgiṇī-mūla* "the root of camphor long-pepper". Since everything around it in 1563 is a ginger-family rhizome, a Zingiberaceae plant under a Kannada name is just as likely.  
* **Do not gather any of these from the wild on the strength of a Sanskrit name.**  
* Two pādas here fall a syllable short of *anuṣṭubh* — 1563a (*māgiṇīmākaṃ paiṣṭuṃ*) and 1564b (*tathā bilakandakam*), seven syllables each against eight — and both defects sit exactly where the unidentifiable words are. Worth collating against Shrigondekar's printed edition before the Devanagari goes public.  
* Turmeric is not in 1561–64 and has been dropped from the ingredient list. Sour turmeric does appear at 1563, but as one of the things being pickled rather than as a spice.  
* On the oil: it is unnamed, and 1562's *sa-rājike taile*, "oil with mustard in it", implies the oil is not itself mustard oil. See the note on the fruit pickles.
