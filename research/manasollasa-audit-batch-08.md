# Mānasollāsa 3.13.1533–1558 — fish, turtle, crab, *mūṣaka*, and the two list-recipes

*Created 2026-07-25 11:49.*

Scope: the seven recipes covering 3.13.1533–1558 — `khara-khanda-salted-fish.md`, `spiced-fish-roe.md`, `nandyavarta-sour-turtle.md`, `ghrta-karkata-crabs-in-ghee.md`, `musaka-style-sour-roast.md`, `sour-tender-leaf-salad.md` (**published**), and `brined-fruits-pods-and-vegetables.md`. All seven were edited. `src/data/sources/manasollasa.ts` was not touched and will need re-syncing.

## Summary

**All seven had substantive errors.** Two are serious enough to change what a cook does.

**The most consequential correction is the crab.** 1540–41 uses *two* vessels — a very hot dry **copper** pan for the roast, then an **earthen** pot for the sour cooking — and the vessel changes at exactly the moment acid enters. Acid standing in unlined copper dissolves copper salts. The reconstruction had collapsed both stages into one pan and added the lemon juice to the same hot vessel, which loses a piece of competent food-safety engineering and would be actively unsafe for anyone who took the source's copper at its word. The method is now split in two, and the reason is stated.

**Second: the turtle was never cut up.** `पक्वरम्भाफलोपमान्` (1537d) is accusative plural masculine agreeing with `कच्छपान्` — the *turtles* resemble ripe plantains once the feet, shell-plates, and head are off, because a dressed turtle body is an oblong smooth lump. There is no cutting verb anywhere in 1537–39, and every referring word downstream stays masculine plural (`सुसिद्धांस्तान्`, `नन्द्यावर्ताः`), one *nandyāvarta* per animal. The old translation invented "cut the flesh into pieces" and re-anchored the comparison to the pieces. Same structure in the crabs (`घृतकर्कटाः`) and the *mūṣakas* (`मूषकेषु`): this run of small-animal recipes cooks the animal whole after removing the appendages.

**Third: the *mūṣaka* is de-haired, not skinned,** and the skin stays on through the roast. 1544 is `रोमाण्युत्पाटयेत्` — "pluck out the hairs" — the same operation as the pig's de-bristling at 1427–29, and 1546 proves the skin is still there by making its drying the doneness test (`यावद्बहिस्त्वचस्तेषां शोषमायान्ति तापनात्`). "Skinned" was wrong. That also explains why the source needs no basting fat, and why the skinless rabbit substitute does.

**Two closing praise-and-property formulas had been dropped wholesale** — 1539's `मृदुरुच्या मनोहराः` and 1542's `वृष्या बलकरा हृद्या मृष्टाः`. The latter four are Ayurvedic property terms (aphrodisiac, strengthening, good for the heart, delicious), not decoration. `मृदु` is cook-facing: tenderness is the stated target for the turtle.

**On the question a correction elsewhere depends on: the *mūṣaka* and *nandyāvarta* sour-cooking readings hold up, with one refinement.** See §8. Both use forms of `पच्` — the plain verb "cook, boil" — with the sours in the instrumental as the medium. There is no way to read either as a marinade. But the pork's verb at 1434 is `परिस्विन्नान्` from `pari-svid`, which is *svedana*, sweating/steaming, not boiling. So the pork correction stands, and if anything the pork wants a gentler, more enclosed moist heat than a full submerged simmer.

**Before cooking anything in this batch, three things worth knowing.** Roe from barbel, tench, gar, and cabezon is toxic independently of freshness and heat does not reliably destroy the toxin. The brine recipe's salt was too low to be reliable and is now raised from ~3% to ~5% water-phase. And the salted fish must be gutted first — that is a safety step, not just a taste step, because uneviscerated salted fish held anaerobically is the classic type E botulism vehicle.

## 1. Salted fish, 1533–1534 (`khara-khanda-salted-fish.md`)

### Confirmed

**K1. `चिरकालं वसन्ति ते` is a clause, not an adjective.** The text makes two statements — they are known as *khara-khaṇḍas*; and they last a long time (`वसन्ति`, 3rd plural present of `vas`). The old translation fused them into "These long-keeping pieces are called *khara-khaṇḍas*". Restored as two clauses.

**K2. The absolute measure is carried, but the conversion was wrong.** `चतुरङ्गुलसम्मितान्` is *carried* — good — but an `aṅgula` is ~1.9 cm, so four of them is ~7.5 cm, and the reconstruction said 8–10 cm. Changed to 7–8 cm, with the conversion in a note. Note also that the text says "measuring four *aṅgulas*" without naming a dimension; "four finger-widths **long**" supplied one. Now "measuring four fingerbreadths".

This is the register point from the brief, and it is worth stating on the page: **this is the only absolute measurement in the whole meat-and-fish run.** Everywhere else the chapter sizes by comparison — chickpea (1449), jujube (1453), large *āmalaka* (1457), camphor lump (1462), areca nut (1473, 1502), sesame (1476). The one other absolute, `अङ्गुलद्वयमानेन` at 1499, is outside this section.

**K3. The gutting prerequisite was missing, and it is a safety step.** 1533 does not repeat the cleaning, because 1524–1528 has just given it in full — scale, behead, de-tail, gut, rub with oil and salt until the slime goes, wash in turmeric water, drain in cloth. This is an unmarked back-reference of exactly the class the brief flags. It matters because the viscera are where salt penetrates last, and uneviscerated salted fish packed into jars is the textbook vehicle for non-proteolytic *C. botulinum* type E. Added to the ingredients ("gutted and scaled") and to the notes.

### Judgement call

**K4. Concord on `सम्मितान्`.** Strictly, `चतुरङ्गुलसम्मितान्` is accusative plural masculine agreeing with `मत्स्यान्` — there is no `खण्डान्` in the verse, only the adverbial `खण्डशः`. So a hyper-literal reading gives "having cut into pieces fish measuring four *aṅgulas*", i.e. small whole fish about 7.5 cm long. **1524 settles it against that reading:** `स्थूलाश्चेत्खण्डशः कार्याः लघवश्चेत्सरूपतः` — large fish get cut up (`खण्डशः`, the same word), small fish are left whole. So `खण्डशः` here signals a large fish, and the modifier goes ad sensum with the pieces. The existing translation was right; I have kept it and left this in the report rather than the page.

### Also changed

- **`खर` glossed in a note.** "Hard, rough, harsh" — the name describes the salt-stiffened texture. The recipe title is otherwise opaque, and the naming formula (`इति ख्याताः`) *is* correctly carried, so the gloss completes it.
- **Salt level made specific.** The old note ("The original jars may have held a much more heavily salted, longer-lasting product") had the right instinct but no numbers. Shelf-stable salt fish needs the expressed brine near saturation: roughly 20–30% salt on the weight of the fish, giving ≥10% in the water phase. The recipe's 8% dry cure for 24–48 h refrigerated is a perfectly good cure and is *not* a preserve; that distinction is now on the page.
- **Turmeric labelled as an addition** — though a well-grounded one, since 1528 washes fish in turmeric water.

## 2. Fish roe, 1535–1536 (`spiced-fish-roe.md`)

### Confirmed

**R1. `आदाय` was dropped, and `कोशौ` is a dual.** `मत्स्याण्डकोशौ आदाय` — "having taken the pair of roe-sacs from the fish". Two losses: the extraction step (the old translation opened straight at "Roast"), and the dual, which is the text noticing that a fish has paired gonads. The unit is one fish's worth of roe. Both restored.

**R2. `परिधूपयेत्` flattened into a sprinkle.** Step 4 read "Sprinkle with pepper, cardamom, asafoetida, and salt". `परि-धूप्` is a fumigation, and the chapter's other recipes get rendered on the site by blooming the aromatic in hot fat and covering the dish. Split into two steps: the dry powders sprinkled, then the asafoetida bloomed in hot ghee, poured over, and covered.

**R3. The intro invented poaching.** "first set by roasting or poaching" — the text has only `वह्निना परिभर्जयेत्`, roasted in the fire. Replaced with the substantive point: setting the sac whole before slicing is what stops the pieces disintegrating in the pan.

### Judgement calls

**R4. Frying oil raised from 30 ml to 60 ml.** `विपच्य तप्ततैले` is a fry in hot oil; 30 ml under 500 g of roe pieces is a thin sauté. 60 ml in a wide pan is a shallow fry, matching how the chapter's other `तप्ततैले` instructions have been read.

**R5. Roe toxicity note added.** The recipe asks for "500 g intact fish roe sacs" of unspecified species. Ichthyootoxism is real and species-specific — barbel (*Barbus*), tench, gar, and cabezon roe are toxic to humans regardless of freshness, and the toxins are at least partly heat-stable. This is the single most likely way to be hurt by this recipe and it was unmentioned. Added, with a list of species whose roe is actually eaten.

The `-आदि` in `एलामरिचकादिभिः` was already carried ("and related spices") — correct, and now named in a note so the openness is explicit. The tray-oil in step 1 is a modern practicality (the text roasts in the fire, with no fat); left in place, since an oven needs it.

## 3. Turtle, 1537–1539 (`nandyavarta-sour-turtle.md`)

### Confirmed

**T1. The plantain comparison was mis-anchored, and a cutting step was invented.** See the summary. `पक्वरम्भाफलोपमान्` agrees with `कच्छपान्`; there is no cutting verb in the passage; `सुसिद्धांस्तान्` (1538) and `नन्द्यावर्ताः` (1539) are both masculine plural, so the referent is the animals throughout. The old translation read "cut the flesh into pieces like ripe banana fruit", which both adds an operation and moves the simile onto it. Rewritten, and the reading is now the intro's lede. Method step 1 keeps the pieces as large as the pan allows and says why.

**T2. `मृदुरुच्या मनोहराः` dropped entirely.** The old translation ended "The dish is called *nandyāvarta*." full stop. Three epithets gone. Restored as "tender, appetizing, and delightful", and `मृदु` promoted to a note because it is the one that tells you what to cook toward.

**T3. `तज्ज्ञः` dropped.** "One who knows the work" — the formulaic expert-cook epithet, which the neighbouring entries sometimes keep. Restored.

**T4. Roasting-first is a technique with a purpose, and it was invisible.** `कच्छपान् वह्निना भृष्ट्वा पादांश्छल्कांश्च मोचयेत्` — you roast in order to *free* (`मोचयेत्`) the plates. Same logic as scalding the pig (1427–28) and scalding the *mūṣaka* (1544). Now stated in the intro. Also corrected the old note's claim that "the source's **slaughter** and shell-removal instructions are not reproduced" — there are no slaughter instructions; 1537 opens at the roast.

### Judgement calls

**T5. The spice powders and the salt are supplied, not sourced.** 1538 says only `चूर्णकैरवचूर्णयेत्`, "dust with powders", and names none. And **1537–39 specifies no salt at all** — unlike the crabs (`लवणाम्लैश्च`, 1541) and the *mūṣaka* (`लवणान्वितैः`, 1545). The cumin/pepper/asafoetida/dried-ginger set is borrowed from 1542, 1547, and 1536. Kept, salt included, but now labelled in a note as borrowed.

**T6. Monkfish removed as a substitute.** The stated target is `मृदु`, tender, and turtle meat is collagen-rich and gelatinous. Monkfish is firm and lean; a 20-minute simmer then a fry gets nowhere near `मृदु`, and the recipe's own doneness language would mislead. Rabbit kept.

### Unresolved

**T7. Why "*nandyāvarta*".** The word names an auspicious diagram — a whorl or interlocking figure, one of the *aṣṭamaṅgala* set — and also a clockwise-spiralled conch and *Tabernaemontana divaricata*. A carapace's scutes do form a concentric figure, but the carapace is removed in this recipe, so that motivation is weak. The text does not say. Kept transliterated with the diagram sense noted and the motivation flagged as unstated.

### Conservation and legality

The existing note was already right to lead with legality, and I have kept its wording. Added: turtles are a well-documented *Salmonella* reservoir, so the raw meat wants raw-poultry handling. That is a cooking hazard the page did not carry.

## 4. Crabs, 1540–1542 (`ghrta-karkata-crabs-in-ghee.md`)

### Confirmed

**C1. The copper→clay vessel switch — the batch's most consequential fix.** 1540 puts the crabs in `रूक्षे ताम्रमये पात्रे सुतप्ते` — a dry copper vessel, very hot. 1541 then says `मृदो भाण्डे विनिक्षिपेत्` — put them into an *earthen* vessel — and only then `विपचेल्लवणाम्लैश्च`, cook with salt and sours.

The translation carried both vessels. **The reconstruction did not.** Its method heated "a heavy dry pan", then added lemon juice to the same pan. Two problems: it loses the technique, and if a reader follows the source's own copper, unlined copper plus 40 ml of lemon juice held hot for 6–10 minutes leaches copper salts. This is why Indian copper cookware is tin-lined. The method is now three steps across two vessels, and the intro says why the switch is there.

Worth noting that this is the same `मृदः स्थाल्यां`/earthenware instruction the prior audit found *dropped* from the sour glaze at 1446. Here the text is even more explicit about it, and here it is load-bearing rather than merely preferable.

**C2. Four dietetic epithets dropped.** `वृष्या बलकरा हृद्या मृष्टास्ते घृतकर्कटाः` — the old translation gave only "These are called *ghṛta-karkaṭas*." `वृष्य` (aphrodisiac), `बलकर` (strengthening), and `हृद्य` (good for the heart) are Ayurvedic property terms placing the dish in a dietetic register; `मृष्ट` is the taste claim. All restored, with a note distinguishing the registers.

**C3. `पुनः सर्पिषि पाचयेत्` is a locative — a second cooking *in* ghee.** The old method treated the ghee as an addition tossed through the finished crab. `सर्पिषि` is locative, so ghee is the medium. Contrast 1538, which uses instrumental `तैलेनाज्येन`. Since the dish is *named* for the ghee, the fry deserves to be a real stage: step 3 now drains the sour liquid, heats the ghee, and fries.

**C4. Asafoetida removed.** 1542's powder list is `जीरकं लवणं तीक्ष्णं चूर्णितं` — three items, **no `-आदि`**, so the list is closed. Asafoetida is in nearly every neighbouring recipe and conspicuously not in this one. Dropped from the ingredients, with the reason noted. Flagged as a judgement call: easy to revert.

### Judgement call

**C5. `तीक्ष्ण` kept as "*tīkṣṇa*" in the translation.** Following the prior audit's finding that this is a named spice in this chapter rather than a generic pungent — it appears in three closed spice lists (1416, 1449, and here) — and most probably black pepper. The old translation's "pungent spice" was defensible; transliterating plus a note is better, because a cook needs a quantity and the ingredient list supplies one.

### Unresolved

**C6. `विवर्जयेत्` (1540d) is unintelligible as transmitted.** It means "he should shun, avoid, abandon" — which cannot be what you do to crabs in a very hot pan. The sense demands a cooking verb, and `विभर्जयेत्` ("he should roast") is the obvious repair: the chapter uses `परिभर्जयेत्` for precisely this at 1534, 1536, and 1549. `विवर्तयेत्` ("turn them about") is the other candidate. **All three scan identically (⏑–⏑–), so the metre cannot decide** — which is exactly why a clean metrical scan does not substitute for collation. The old translation silently supplied "Heat"; the page now carries a bracketed editorial note per house convention, and I have not touched the Devanagari.

**C7. `खपर` (1541a) is a vernacular form.** `स्फोटिते खपरे` — "when the shell has cracked". Sanskrit `कर्पर` is "a shell (of a tortoise or crab), a potsherd, a skull"; `खपर` looks like the Prakritic/vernacular form behind Hindi *khappar*, a potsherd or skull. Both scan (`खपरे` ⏑⏑–, `कर्परे` –⏑–, and position 5 stays light either way). Noted on the page. This is the second vernacular item in the stretch, after `मयिग` at 1543 — the chapter does admit regional vocabulary.

`हस्तपादवियोजितान्` was already well handled by the existing note, which I kept verbatim.

## 5. *Mūṣaka*, 1543–1547 (`musaka-style-sour-roast.md`)

### Confirmed

**M1. "Skinned" is wrong: the animal is de-haired.** `रोमाण्युत्पाटयेत्` — pluck out the hairs — after a scald, exactly as the pig is de-bristled at 1428–29 (which the site's own translation there renders "remove the bristles"). And 1546 confirms the skin is still present by making its drying the doneness test: `यावद्बहिस्त्वचस्तेषां शोषमायान्ति तापनात्`, "until their outer skin dries from the heating". Corrected in the translation and noted.

This has a downstream consequence. **1546 adds no fat**, and the old step 3 said "Brush with ghee and roast" — which works directly against the text's own doneness cue. But the substitute animal arrives skinless and very lean, so removing the ghee outright would give leathery rabbit. Step 3 now brushes lightly and says in-line why the source doesn't need to.

**M2. `पुच्छधारणात्` dropped.** "By holding the tail" — you hold the animal by the tail to dip it in the scalding water. This is the passage's most specific piece of technique and the old translation had nothing but "is scalded". Restored, along with `उद्धृत्य तस्मात्सलिलात्`, "lift it out of that water".

**M3. Two conjoined habitat facts were turned into a disjunction.** `क्षेत्रसम्भूता ... नदीकूलेषु संस्थिताः` are both nominative plural adjectives of `मूषकाः`, asyndetically conjoined — field-bred *and* living along river banks. There is no `वा`. The old translation gave "a field **or** river animal", which converts one animal's two habitats into two candidate animals, and directly feeds the "identification is uncertain" framing. Fixed.

**M4. The uncertainty was misassigned — the identification is good.** The old intro said "The animal identification is uncertain and may denote a large field or water rodent." But 1543 is unusually diagnostic: large, dark, well-fed, field-bred, riverbank-dwelling, and given a vernacular species name. Those are the field marks of the greater bandicoot rat, *Bandicota indica*, which is a large (to ~1 kg) dark field rodent of Indian cropland and riverbanks and is still eaten in parts of India. **The site's own source blurb already says "*mūṣaka*, probably the bandicoot rat"** — so the blurb and the recipe were contradicting each other, with the recipe the more timid of the two. Rewritten: the species is reasonably secure, the substitution is about supply.

The genuinely unidentified item is `मयिग`, the vernacular name at 1543d (`जात्या ते मयिगाः स्मृताः`, "by their kind they are called *mayigas*"). Someśvara III ruled from Kalyāṇa, so a Kannada or Deccan regional term is expected, but I could not match `mayiga` to a modern word. The old translation's "called *mūṣaka* **or** *mayiga*" implied two synonyms; the text marks one as the Sanskrit term and one as the local name.

### The verdict the pork correction depends on — see §8.

### Judgement calls

**M5. The animals are spitted whole.** `शूलपोतांस्ततः कृत्वा तानङ्गारैः प्रतापयेत्` — "then, having made them spit-transfixed, roast them over coals", with `तान्` masculine plural = the *mūṣakas*. Same whole-animal pattern as the turtle and the crabs. Jointing a rabbit into eight is a convenience and I have left it, with a note.

**M6. `स्फोटयेदन्त्रकाणि`.** I read this as its own clause — "clear out the entrails" — with `पचेत्` in the next pāda taking the animals as its unexpressed object. The alternative reads `अन्त्रकाणि` as what gets cooked, making this a chitterlings recipe; the circulating English paraphrase (see §7) takes it that way. **1547's locative absolute `सुपक्वेषु ... मूषकेषु`, "when those *mūṣakas* are well cooked", settles it** — the whole animals are the thing cooked. Noted here rather than on the page.

### Unresolved

**M7. `शूलपोतान्` is probably for `शूलप्रोतान्`.** `पोत` means a young animal, a boat, or cloth — none of which fit. `प्रोत` is the standard participle "pierced, strung, transfixed" (as in `सूत्रप्रोत`, strung on a thread), and `शूलप्रोत` is an attested compound. A dropped `र`. `शूलपोतांस्` and `शूलप्रोतांस्` scan identically (– ⏑ – –), so again the metre is blind to it. The translation's "threaded on skewers" was already right; noted on the page, Devanagari untouched.

## 6. Leaf salad, 1550–1553 — **published** (`sour-tender-leaf-salad.md`)

### Does the compression lose anything a cook needs?

**Mostly no, and Damon's framing is right.** The list's function *is* "any tender sour leaf", the recipe is a dressing not a composition, and spinach is a perfectly good answer. I have not touched the intro, the ingredients, or the method.

But it lost three things worth having:

1. **`सुरसाद्वयम्` — "the two *surasā*s", i.e. both kinds of basil.** A dropped quantifier (`द्वयम्`) *and* the most immediately cookable idea in the list. Basil leaves in a sour salad is something a reader can act on tonight. The same `-द्वयम्` formula recurs at 1557 (`बृहतीद्वयम्`), so it is a standing convention in the chapter, not a one-off.
2. **`भल्लातक` was named in the safety note but absent from the translation** — the note warned about a plant the page never showed. Now named in both.
3. **`तथापरम्`** — the list closes with "and another besides", which is the text explicitly declaring itself open-ended. Restored.

### Is the safety note accurate? Yes — and it understated one thing.

*Bhallātaka* is *Semecarpus anacardium*. Its tarry sap contains **bhilawanol**, a mixture of 3-*n*-pentadec(en)yl catechols, which are **urushiols** — the same chemical class as the poison-ivy allergen — plus anacardic acid. It blisters on contact. It is classed under *upaviṣa* in Ayurveda, listed as a poisonous plant under India's Drugs and Cosmetics Act, and requires *śodhana* before internal use; there is published clinical literature on dermatitis acquired *during* the purification procedure. So "can be irritant or toxic if improperly handled" was correct and if anything mild. Sharpened with the mechanism.

Two hazards the note missed and now carries: **`कोकिलाक्षी` (*Hygrophila auriculata*) is spiny**, with axillary thorns; and **flax carries cyanogenic glycosides** (linamarin, linustatin) in seed and vegetative tissue — documented in livestock forage poisoning. Both calibrated, neither overstated.

I also added the plain point the note was missing per the brief: **buy the leaves, don't gather them.** The old wording ("use only leaves positively identified as edible") reads as careful-foraging advice.

## 7. Brine list, 1554–1558 (`brined-fruits-pods-and-vegetables.md`)

### Confirmed

**B1. The translation was a précis, not a translation.** "A long list of fruits, pods, aromatics, gourds, grapes, eggplants, limes, citron, *āmalaka*, mango, *niṣpāva*, and other produce is to be placed in salt water." — **11 items out of about 40**, reordered, with category labels ("fruits, pods, aromatics, gourds") that are not in the text. Replaced with a full enumeration in text order, glossing what is secure and transliterating what is not.

**B2. The enumeration has two groups, not one.** `लवणाम्भसि निक्षिपेत्` closes 1556, and `लवणाम्भसि` closes 1558 with the verb understood. So the text gives **two** brine lists, and they are differently weighted: 1554–56 runs to aromatics, pods, and medicinal fruits (bael, screw pine, tamarind, *kuṭaja*, pepper, *pathyā*, cardamom, asafoetida, fenugreek, *agasti*, *karīra*, tendu, cane); 1557–58 to ordinary produce (mango, hog plum, *āmalaka*, gourds, cucumber, grapes, the two *bṛhatīs*, luffa, citron, lablab, karonda, lime, ivy gourd, eggplant). The old page collapsed the structure. Now carried, and the grouping is the intro's substance.

**B3. `बृहतीद्वयम्` — the quantifier dropped.** "The two *bṛhatīs*", the standard thesaurus pairing of *Solanum indicum* and *S. xanthocarpum*. Restored, and noted alongside `सुरसाद्वयम्` at 1551 as a recurring convention.

**B4. `निष्पाव` — the translation was right, the ingredients were wrong.** The `translation` field correctly left it transliterated. But the ingredient list offered **"green beans"** — *Phaseolus vulgaris*, a New World plant unavailable in 12th-century India, and not lablab in any case. Corrected to "tender lablab (*sem*) pods", with a note giving the identification (*Lablab purpureus*; the young pod eaten whole), the two things it is not (fava, green beans), and flat romano beans as the practical substitute. This matches the prior audit's correction at 1451 and confirms it independently: 1558 is the brine-list occurrence that audit cited, and 1365–66 cooks `निष्पावा` whole (`अभिन्नाः`) rather than split.

### Judgement calls

**B5. Salt raised from 60 g to 100 g.** With 1 kg of produce (~900 g of its own water) and 1 L of added water, 60 g gives about **3.1%** salt in the water phase — low for a room-temperature vegetable ferment, with real spoilage and pectinolytic-softening risk. 100 g gives **5.0%**, which is a reliable lactic fermentation. Fermentation time stretched 2–5 days → 3–7 to match the slower ferment, "until the brine has definitely turned sour" added as the endpoint, and "cover the jar loosely" added because a sealed jar builds pressure.

The old note ("The source may describe preservation in plain strong brine rather than active fermentation") had exactly the right instinct, and my reading confirms it: `लवणाम्भस्` is **salt-stock brining** — preservation by osmotic dehydration at 10%+ water-phase salt, needing a freshwater soak before eating — not a short 3% ferment. What the note lacked was the number that makes the difference. Now on the page, along with the plain statement that this recipe makes a refrigerator pickle rather than a preserve.

Worth knowing: several listed items are themselves acidic — tamarind, bael, karonda, lime, citron, *āmalaka* — so the historical brine was probably partly self-acidifying.

**B6. Mustard removed; fenugreek kept.** "Optional: 1 tsp mustard seed and ½ tsp fenugreek seed". **Mustard is not in 1554–58** — `राजिका` belongs to 1559–1560, the *next* recipe, which is an oil-and-mustard pickle. Removed, with a note. **Fenugreek is legitimate**: `मेथिका` is right there at 1555a. But note the ambiguity I initially misjudged — see below.

### The aromatics: an ambiguity worth flagging rather than resolving

I first read the reconstruction's fenugreek-in-the-brine as a mis-anchoring, on the grounds that `मेथिका` is one of the accusative items being *put into* the brine. On reflection that is not decidable. Everything in both lists is accusative and governed by the single verb `निक्षिपेत्`, so cardamom, asafoetida, pepper, and fenugreek are grammatically on all fours with the mangoes and the gourds. Whether they are things being pickled or things flavouring the pickle, the syntax is identical. The honest treatment is to say so, which the intro now does. Fenugreek stays in the ingredients.

### Unresolved

**B7. `विषमुष्टिकशिम्बिजम्` (1554d) — a possible toxin in the list.** `विषमुष्टि` normally denotes ***Strychnos nux-vomica***, and `शिम्बिज` is "pod-born", so "the pods of *viṣamuṣṭika*". Nux-vomica is a strychnine plant, used in Ayurveda only after *śodhana*. Confidence in the identification is medium at best, but this is not a name to gloss silently: the translation now carries it with a bracketed note naming the plant, its toxicity, and the insecurity of the reading. It is not in the ingredients and should not be.

**B8. `कर्कटी` appears twice within verse 1557** — once at 1557b (`कुहिरि कर्कटी तथा`) and again at 1557d (`कर्कटी बृहतीद्वयम्`). Possible dittography. Metrically, 1557b *requires* `कर्कटी` (substituting `कर्कोटी`, *Momordica dioica*, would make position 5 heavy and break the mandatory light 5th syllable), but 1557d would take `कर्कोटी` without damage — and spine gourd is a standard Indian pickling vegetable, so that would both remove the duplication and add a plausible item. Flagged for collation; Devanagari untouched. Bracketed note on the page.

**B9. Eight names I could not identify** and have left transliterated: `सुगन्धिज`, `नीवार` (glossed "wild rice", secure enough to bracket), `पर्पट` (ambiguous between the herb *pittapāpaḍā* and the crisp wafer of 1549), `नन्दन`, `कट`, `मद`, `कर्कट`, `कारीफल`, `कुहिरि`, `कर्मर`. `कुहिरि` does not look Sanskrit and is probably a vernacular plant name, like `मयिग` at 1543. `वेत्रकारीफलं` may segment as `वेत्र` + `कारीफल` or otherwise; I took the former.

### Items added to the ingredients

`करमर्दक` (karonda, *Carissa carandas*), `बिम्ब` (tindora / ivy gourd, *Coccinia grandis*), and `कूष्माण्ड` (ash gourd) — all high-confidence, all readily available, all previously absent from a list of "or similar". Three more, noted rather than listed because they are worth knowing about: `मरिच` here is **green peppercorns**, which brine very well; `करीर` is *Capparis decidua*, the desert caper whose buds are still a standard Rajasthani brine pickle; `केतक` is the screw pine, whose tender white heart is eaten.

## 8. Verdict: do the *mūṣaka* and *nandyāvarta* sour-cooking readings hold?

**Yes. Both are sound, and the pork correction that leans on them stands.** With one refinement that is worth carrying back.

| | Verse | Construction | Verb | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Mūṣaka* | 1545d | `सम्भारसहितैरम्लैः पचेच्च लवणान्वितैः` | **`पचेत्`** — bare optative of `pac` | "cook with sours, seasoned and salted" |
| *Nandyāvarta* | 1538a | `अम्लकैश्च विपच्याथ` | **`विपच्य`** — gerund of `vi-pac` | "having cooked thoroughly with sours" |
| Crabs (control) | 1541c | `विपचेल्लवणाम्लैश्च` | **`विपचेत्`** — optative of `vi-pac` | "cook with salt and sours" |

`पच्` is *the* Sanskrit verb for cooking and boiling. There is no marinade sense available to it, and in all three cases the sours stand in the instrumental as the medium. The crabs at 1541 are an independent third witness to the identical construction in the same run of verses, and the crab passage puts the sours into a *pot* (`मृदो भाण्डे`) over heat, which nails down that the instrumental sours are a cooking liquid. Both site translations render this as a braise and both are right.

**The refinement.** The pork's verb is a different word. 1434c is `अथाम्लपरिस्विन्नान्` — a participle of `pari-svid`, the *svedana* family: sweating, steaming, sudation. That is moist heat, so the correction from a cold marinade to a cooking step is right, and the *mūṣaka* and *nandyāvarta* are legitimate models for the *shape* of the operation (moist-heat pre-cook in sour liquid → drain → skewer → fire). But `pari-svid` is not `pac`. Strictly it points to a gentler, more enclosed operation — a covered sweat in a smaller volume of liquid, its own juices included — rather than pieces submerged in a simmering pan. If the pork recipe has been given a full 350 ml submerged simmer on this authority, that is defensible by analogy but slightly over-reads the verb; a tightly covered sweat with less liquid is closer to what 1434 actually says. The chapter distinguishes these words: `स्विन्न` recurs at 1436 (`स्विन्नानां शुण्ठकानां`) while `पच्` runs through 1538, 1541, and 1545.

So: **no need to revisit the pork's direction of travel. Worth revisiting its liquid volume.**

A single long vowel. I have **not** emended the Devanagari; the translation carries a bracketed note.

## 10. Cross-cutting findings

Three patterns that recur across my seven and are worth checking in the other batches.

**The closing epithet formula is systematically dropped.** 1539 (`मृदुरुच्या मनोहराः`) and 1542 (`वृष्या बलकरा हृद्या मृष्टाः`) both lost every epithet. The prior audit found the same at 1435 (`अमृतोपमान्`). The site handles it correctly at 1461 (`वरम्` → "This excellent preparation is called puryala"), so the convention exists — it is just applied unevenly. Worth a single sweep. Note that `वृष्य`, `बलकर`, and `हृद्य` are Ayurvedic property terms rather than praise, and the source array's own translations drop them at 1515 and 1517 too.

**The whole-animal reading.** 1537–39 (turtle), 1540–42 (crabs), and 1543–47 (*mūṣaka*) all cook the animal entire after removing appendages, and all three keep the referent in the masculine plural through to the dish name. Any recipe in this run that has been rendered as "cut into pieces" without a cutting verb in the Sanskrit deserves a second look.

**Vernacular vocabulary.** `मयिग` (1543), `खपर` (1541), and probably `कुहिरि` (1557) are regional rather than Sanskrit forms. Given a Chalukya author at Kalyāṇa, this is expected, and it means an obscure word in this chapter is not automatically corrupt — it may be Kannada or Deccan Prakrit. Useful prior for the other batches.

## 11. A published paraphrase exists — the source blurb is now slightly out of date

`src/content/sources/manasollasa.md` says "unfortunately no scholarly translation into English exists." That is still true of a *scholarly* translation, but a paraphrase of at least the *mūṣaka* passage circulates in English, apparently deriving from P. Arundhati's *Royal Life in Mānasôllāsa*, and it independently confirms two of my corrections:

> the rats, which are strong and black, born in the fields and along river banks, are called maiga; these are fried in hot oil holding with the tail till the hair is removed … the stomach is cut and the inner parts are cooked with amla and salt; or the rat is kept on iron rods and fired on red hot coal, till outer skin is burnt or shrinks.

**Confirms:** holding by the tail (`पुच्छधारणात्`), hair removal rather than skinning (`रोमाण्युत्पाटयेत्`), the skin still present at the roast, and iron rods for `शूल`.

**Where it is wrong and the site should not follow it:** "fried in hot **oil**" — `प्रतप्ते सलिले` is *heated water*, and 1544c repeats `तस्मात्सलिलात्`, "out of that water", so the scald is unambiguous. And "**or** the rat is kept on iron rods" — `ततः` is "then", a sequence, not an alternative; the sour cooking and the roast are consecutive stages, which is the whole basis of the braise-then-fire reading. Damon may want to note in the blurb that a partial English paraphrase exists, and that it is loose. I have not edited the blurb — not my file.

Arundhati's volume is the obvious thing to consult if it can be had; it would also settle 1449a for the prior audit and the four collation items in §9.
