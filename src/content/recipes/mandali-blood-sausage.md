Blood, fat, and spices with no filler at all, run into gut and coiled into rings that the text says look like the long dark pods of the golden shower tree. The rings are tied shut with lengths of gut used as string, and then picked up by those to be held over the coals.

## Ingredients

* 500 ml food-grade pig or lamb blood  
* 200 g pork back fat or lamb fat, finely diced  
* 40 g lemon or citron pulp, finely chopped  
* 20 ml lime juice, for the *dantaśaṭha*  
* 30 ml ginger juice  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* 1 tsp ground coriander  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 2 tsp rock salt  
* 2 m cleaned sausage casing, plus a spare length for tying

## Method

1. Keep everything cold. Strain the blood through a fine sieve to catch the clots — this is the *pūrvavat śodhite* of 1494, the purification the text sets out at 1469–70.  
2. Whisk the strained blood smooth, stir in the citrus, ginger juice, spices, and salt, then fold in the diced fat. Add the citrus last and fill promptly; juice standing in raw blood starts to set it.  
3. Fill the casing loosely, leaving room for expansion, and coil it into rings. Tie the ends shut with a length of casing, as the text does, or with twine.  
4. Poach in water held at 80–85°C until firm and the centre reaches at least 72°C, about 25–40 minutes depending on thickness.  
5. Cool briefly, then grill or roast over embers until the casing is browned.

**Notes**

* The source heats the suspended coils over embers; controlled poaching followed by grilling greatly reduces bursting.  
* ***Dantaśaṭha* is a citrus, most likely the lime**, so it should not be dropped. Monier-Williams glosses the word — literally "tooth-blunting" — as the common lime, *Citrus acida*; the minority glosses (wood-apple, orange, carambola) are all sour fruit. 1494 asks for two separate citrus elements, citron pulp and *dantaśaṭha* juice, which is why both are in the list.  
* **"Purified as before" points at a specific procedure.** *Pūrvavac chodhite rakte* (1494) refers back to 1469–70, where the blood is caught in a pot, lightly salted, worked with the fingers, and the fibrous clots lifted out. That is step 1, and it is not cosmetic: blood that has not been de-fibrinated makes a grainy sausage.  
* **No filler.** The passage has blood, fat, and spices, and nothing else — no rice, groats, or bread. So the result is dense and firm, nearer a very pure boudin noir than anything with grain in it. 200 g of fat to 500 ml of blood is on the lean side of traditional practice; more fat gives a softer sausage.  
* *Kamrā* is simply "lovely", an adjective doing the work of a noun; the noun it implies is presumably *maṇḍalī* itself, the ring the dish is named for. 1497 then speaks of the "mouths" of the rings, meaning the two open ends, tied together with gut.  
* **The embers are specified by color.** *Aṅgāraiḥ kiṃśukākāraiḥ* — embers in the form of *kiṃśuka* blossoms, the brilliant orange-red flowers of *Butea monosperma*. The comparison describes the coals rather than the sausage, and what it specifies is glowing red-orange charcoal with no flame left on it.  
* Citron is hard to find; lemon pulp with a little finely grated peel is the closest substitute.  
* Use only inspected culinary blood and properly prepared casings. Blood is an excellent medium for bacteria: keep it at or below 4°C, use it within a day or two of collection, and do not let the filled sausage stand at room temperature before poaching.
