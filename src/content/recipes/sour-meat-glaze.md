  He should mix tamarind into curd, or else pomegranate essence; or he should combine ground emblic myrobalan, or *amlavetasa*; or the juice of citron, and the juice of *dantaśaṭha*. Or he may mix these juices, two or three of them, as he pleases.

This is the sauce the text puts the preceding roast pork *śuṇṭhakas* into. 1447 says only "throw the *śuṇṭhakas* in there", and the word *māṃsa* never appears in the passage, so it reads as the finish for that roast rather than a free-standing dish.

## Ingredients

* 500 g cooked roast pork, lamb, or goat  
* 300 g plain yoghurt  
* 30 g tamarind concentrate  
* 40 ml pomegranate molasses, or 100 ml pomegranate juice reduced to 40 ml  
* 30 ml lemon juice  
* 1 tsp ground coriander  
* 1 tsp ground cumin  
* ½ tsp turmeric  
* 15 g fresh ginger, very finely cut  
* ½ tsp ground dried ginger  
* ½ tsp ground black pepper  
* ¼ tsp asafoetida  
* 1½ tsp rock salt  
* 20 ml sesame oil or neutral oil  
* To finish: ¼ tsp asafoetida, ½ tsp cumin seeds, 1 tsp ghee

## Method

1. Bring the yoghurt to room temperature and whisk it smooth on its own.  
2. Add the tamarind, pomegranate, lemon juice, spices, both gingers, and salt, and whisk to combine.  
3. Strain through muslin or a fine cloth.  
4. Stir in the oil, after the straining rather than before.  
5. Cook in a heavy non-reactive pan over low heat, stirring more or less continuously, until it thickens to a glaze heavy enough to coat the back of a spoon — 30–45 minutes in a wide pan, longer in a narrow one. Do not let it boil. It is ready when the oil starts to bead at the edges.  
6. Add the cooked meat and simmer gently for 15–25 minutes, until the sauce clings to the pieces. Add water only if it threatens to scorch before the meat is soft.  
7. Take the pan off the heat, then finish it: either bloom the asafoetida and cumin seeds in the hot ghee and stir them through, or fumigate it with smoke as the text asks — see the notes.

**Notes**

* **The sours.** The text offers six — tamarind, pomegranate essence, ground *āmalaka*, *amlavetasa*, citron juice, and *dantaśaṭha* juice — and says to use one or mix two or three (*dvi-trān*, 1443). This version uses three. *Dantaśaṭha* is most likely lime, so the lemon juice stands in for it as well as for the citron. *Āmalaka* is omitted: *piṣṭaṃ* is a fresh ground pulp, and dried amla powder is mostly tannin and fibre, which binds the curd protein and turns the sauce astringent. Three of the six are pulps that bring body — tamarind, *āmalaka*, *amlavetasa* — and three are juices that do not; none of the six brings starch. *Amlavetasa* is contested — Garcinia pedunculata in Bengal and Assam, Himalayan rhubarb stalk elsewhere — and omitted.  
* **Curd is the bulk of it, and the grammar is not what settles that.** 1442 puts *dadhi* in the instrumental and the sours in the accusative, which fixes the direction — the sours go into the curd — but an instrumental carries no proportion, and 1477 runs the same verb the other way round, with the meat accusative and 15 g of ginger instrumental. What settles it is that the text lets you use a *single* sour: 1442–43 offers six alternatives and only then licenses combining two or three. Curd plus lime juice plus spices still has to reduce to something lickable, and that only works if the curd carries the body. An earlier version of this reconstruction had 100 g of yoghurt against 260 ml of acid, which is a fruit sauce with a little yoghurt in it.  
* **Keep it short on the heat.** Yoghurt is about 85% water, so a bigger curd share means *more* to evaporate, not less: 300 g of yoghurt against 100 ml of sours is roughly 250 ml of water to drive off before the meat goes in. Reducing the pomegranate separately first, and using tamarind concentrate rather than soaked pulp, is what holds that to half an hour. Salt is the one thing working against you — 1½ tsp is about 2% here, enough to undo the electrostatic repulsion that keeps casein dispersed this far below its isoelectric point. The text puts the salt in early (1445) and I have left it there, but holding a third of it back until the sauce has thickened costs nothing.  
* **Straining is load-bearing, not cosmetic.** *Gālayet sita-vastreṇa* (1445) is a plain optative like every other instruction here. Passing the curd through cloth homogenizes the gel and removes the tamarind fibre, seed grit, and ginger fibre that would otherwise read as a split sauce. The oil goes in afterwards, following the text's order, and it is the real safeguard: there is no starch anywhere in this recipe, and what keeps a reduced curd sauce smooth is a continuous fat phase holding the protein solids — the same reason a *dahi* masala reads smooth once it has cooked out into its fat. Note the text asks for *taila*, oil, not ghee. If the sauce does grain, a second pass through the cloth recovers it.  
* *Sāraka* in *dāḍima-sāraka* means essence or pith, and the next verse switches to *rasa* for plain juice, so the pomegranate is probably a concentrate. Molasses is closer than supermarket juice; *anārdāna*, dried sour pomegranate seed, would be closer still.  
* The text marks its purposes: coriander, asafoetida, and cumin are *gandhārthaṃ*, for aroma; turmeric is *varṇārthaṃ*, for colour only, which is why half a teaspoon is right; the dried ginger, pepper, and salt are *rucyarthaṃ*, for relish.  
* **How thick.** *Pralehaka* (1446) places this in the *lehya* class of the fivefold scheme at 1344–45, food that is licked. The chapter's own yardstick is 1375, milk-pudding thickness. 1452, which reuses this sauce, ends *śoṣite 'mlarase*, "when the sour juice has dried away" — so the family cooks down to nearly dry, not to a curry gravy.  
* The text specifies an earthen pot (*mṛdaḥ sthālyām*). Any heavy non-reactive pan will do; avoid unlined reactive metal with a sauce this acidic.  
* **The finish is smoke, and the text means it literally.** *Dhūpayet* (1448) is an instruction rather than an option, and 1447 has already lifted the pot off the fire — off the heat, then smoke. The give-away is *dhūpena jīrakasya*, "with the smoke *of* cumin", where cumin is a genitive hanging off *dhūpa*: the thing in the instrumental is the smoke itself. The text names asafoetida, onycha (*nakhadhūpa*, the dried operculum of a marine snail, sold as *nakhla*), cumin, and probably camphor — and onycha cannot be eaten at all, which settles the reading for the whole list. To do it: stand a small cup on the sauce, drop in a lump of glowing charcoal, put the asafoetida or cumin on the coal with a few drops of ghee, cover the pan for five minutes, and lift the cup out. The hot-ghee bloom in step 7 is a substitute for that rather than a translation of it — though a period-appropriate one, since the chapter's own puryala (1459–60) heats ghee separately and throws asafoetida into it.  
* Citron is hard to find; lemon pulp with a little finely grated peel is the closest substitute.  
* The text gives this dish no name — unlike puryala, bhaditraka, or kavacandī, which it names explicitly. "Glaze" is the reconstructor's word; *Pralehaka* would be the source-anchored title.
