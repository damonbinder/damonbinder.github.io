A close relative of medu vada. Two things differ: the several holes are decorative rather than a single central opening, and the batter is the soured *iḍerikā* batter rather than the freshly ground, unfermented urad a medu vada uses.

## Ingredients

* 1 batch fermented iḍerikā batter (makes about 8)  
* 700 ml neutral oil, for frying  
* Water, for wetting the hands

## Method

1. Heat the oil to 165–170°C.  
2. Wet one hand and spread about 80 g batter into a disc roughly 9 cm across and 1.5–2 cm thick, on the palm or on a piece of baking parchment.  
3. Make five or seven holes, about 1 cm wide, with a wet fingertip or a chopstick.  
4. Slide carefully into the oil and fry until deep golden to reddish brown, 6–8 minutes, turning once.  
5. Drain on a rack and serve hot.

**Notes**

* A single central hole is easier, but the source explicitly describes five or seven holes. On a disc this broad and thick they also do real work: they cut the distance heat has to travel, which is what lets the middle cook before the outside goes past red.  
* The batter is the fermented one. *Tasyaiva māṣapiṣṭasya* (1401) — "of that very same black-gram batter" — points back to the *āmlībhūtaṃ māṣapiṣṭaṃ*, the soured batter, of 1399. That is the main departure from medu vada, whose batter is ground and beaten but not fermented. If a fermented batch has slackened too much to hold a punched hole, it was ground too wet or left too long.  
* *Bhakṣyeṣu* (1403) places these in the *bhakṣya* class — morsel or snack foods — the second of the five kinds of food set out at 1344: *bhojya*, *bhakṣya*, *peya*, *lehya*, *coṣya*. The chapter uses the term sparingly and it is worth keeping.  
* Use a thermometer: thick urad batter can brown outside before the centre is cooked.  
* One batch of *iḍerikā* batter makes about eight of these, which is also what the two *vaṭaka* recipes that follow assume. The steamed *iḍerikās* and the fried *ghārikās* are alternative uses of one batch, not two batches.
