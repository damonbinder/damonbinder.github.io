The name is the recipe: *dhūpa* is smoke, and what separates this from the plain *kāñjika* the king drinks at the end of the meal (1597) is that this one has been salted and put under aromatic smoke. The verse assumes you already have the sour ferment in the pantry and says nothing about making it, so everything below the salt is reconstruction.

## Ingredients

**For the *sauvīra*** (start 3–4 days ahead)

* 150 g pearl barley  
* 1.5 L water  
* 2 tbsp of an existing sour ferment as a starter — leftover *kāñjika*, sour whey, or live cultured buttermilk  
* ½ tsp salt

**For the drink**

* 500 ml clear sour *sauvīra*, from above  
* ¾ tsp rock salt  
* ½ tsp cumin seeds and ¼ tsp asafoetida, for the smoke  
* A small piece of lump charcoal

## Method

1. **Ferment.** Simmer the barley in the water until soft, about 40 minutes, and let it cool to lukewarm. Tip grain and liquid into a clean 2 L glass or glazed jar, stir in the starter and the salt, and cover loosely so gas can escape. Keep it at 22–28 °C out of direct sun, stir once a day with a clean spoon, and keep the grain pushed under the liquid. It will be sharply sour in 2–4 days.  
2. **Clarify.** Strain through muslin, refrigerate overnight, then decant the clear liquid off the sediment. This is the verse's `निर्मलं`.  
3. Stir the rock salt into 500 ml of the clear ferment.  
4. **Fumigate.** Stand a small metal cup in the middle of the bowl so its rim clears the surface. Light the charcoal until it glows, drop it in, scatter the cumin seeds and asafoetida over it with a few drops of ghee, and cover the vessel tightly for 4–5 minutes. Lift the cup out.  
5. Serve cold or at room temperature.

**Notes**

* **The smoke is the dish.** `धूपितं` is not an optional flourish here — it is in the name. Whisking powdered asafoetida and roasted cumin into the liquid gives you a salted *kāñjika*, which is what 1404 already describes; it does not give you a *dhūpa-kāñjika*.  
* **What `धूप` means, since the other recipes point here.** Throughout this chapter — about thirty occurrences — `धूप` is **smoke applied to the food from outside it**: the aromatic is burned, and the vessel is covered so the food takes the scent. It is not an ingredient that goes into the pot. Three independent things establish it. `नखधूप` at 1448 is onycha, the dried operculum of a marine snail burned as incense, which cannot be eaten at all. `कर्पूरधूपितं` at 1573 is camphor, which is a sublimating solid nobody wants a spoonful of — as smoke it is exactly right, and it also solves the problem of finding camphor you trust to eat. And 1478 puts the two treatments of one substance in adjacent pādas: `हिङ्गुधूपेन वासयेत्`, scent it with asafoetida-incense, against `हिङ्गुतोयेन सिञ्चितम्`, sprinkled with asafoetida-water. The chapter keeps `हिङ्गुतोय` (six times, thrown in), `हिङ्गुधूप` (three times, burned), and `हिङ्गुसर्पिस्` (once, at 1400, dripped onto the fumigant) apart, so a translation that renders all three as "add asafoetida" is throwing away distinctions the author was making.  
* **Both aromatics go on the coal.** `हिङ्गुना जीरकेणापि धूपितं` puts asafoetida and cumin in the same instrumental phrase governed by `धूपितं`, with `अपि` adding the cumin to the asafoetida. Compare 1400, `हिङ्गुसर्पिर्भ्यां जीरकेण च धूपयेत्` — asafoetida with ghee, and cumin, as the fumigant. The ghee in that verse is what makes the smoke, not a frying medium.  
* ***Sauvīra* is a barley ferment, and probably not the same thing as *kāñjika*.** In the Ayurvedic literature on fermented preparations, `sauvīraka` and `tuṣodaka` are the barley sours — distinguished from each other by whether the husk is kept — while `kāñjika` / `dhānyāmla` is the rice sour. Reading 1580 that way makes the verse informative: take the barley ferment, clarify it, salt it, smoke it, and you have a *kāñjika*. [Judgement call: barley is the recommended reading but it comes from the wider Ayurvedic literature, not from this text. Clear strained rice *kāñjika* works and is the fallback.]  
* ***Sauvīra* here is not the jujube.** It is also the name of the largest grade in the Ayurvedic *badara* triad — *sauvīra* / *kola* / *karkandhu* — but `निर्मलं`, "clear, free of sediment", is a liquid word, used in this chapter of cloth (1413), water (1431), and a liquid coming clear (1583). A fruit is not `निर्मल`.  
* **Fermentation safety.** The hazard window is the first day or two, before the acid builds. The starter is what shortens it, so do not skip it. Cooked grain held warm is the classic vehicle for *Bacillus cereus*, whose spores survive boiling, so get the barley into acid promptly and ferment at room temperature rather than somewhere warm. Keep everything submerged — anything sitting above the liquid will mould. A white film of yeast can be skimmed; fuzzy or coloured mould, a solvent smell, or anything putrid means the batch goes out. pH strips are cheap and are the only real check: below 4.0 before you drink it. Ferment in glass, glazed ceramic, or food-grade plastic, never bare reactive metal. Refrigerate once it is sour enough; it keeps a couple of weeks cold and goes on souring slowly.  
* **This entry is the chapter's authority on salting *kāñjika*.** 1404 puts *vaṭakas* into *kāñjika* and specifies no seasoning at all; the salt in that recipe is borrowed from here. 1405–07 wants its *kāñjika* thick and unstrained for the starch, which is the opposite of the `निर्मलं` asked for here — the two uses pull in different directions, so keep them as separate batches.
